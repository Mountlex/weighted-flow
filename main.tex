\title{Weighted Flow Time Does Not Admit\\ $O(1)$-Competitive Randomized Algorithms}

\author{%

\date{}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We study the classical online problem of scheduling jobs with release times, sizes, and weights on a single machine with preemption so as to minimize the total weighted flow time. Bansal and Chan (SODA~2009) showed that no deterministic online algorithm is $O(1)$-competitive for this problem. We show that randomization does not overcome this barrier: no randomized online algorithm is $O(1)$-competitive against an oblivious adversary. Quantitatively, every randomized algorithm has competitive ratio $\Omega(\min\{\log\log W,\log\log\log P\})$, where $W$ and $P$ are the ratios between the largest and the smallest weight and size, respectively.

Following Yao's principle, we construct a single distribution over instances that defeats every deterministic algorithm. The construction compares the unfinished weight of the algorithm with that of an optimal schedule at a random time that the algorithm cannot observe. Starting from a single job, it alternates two operations. Amplification appends a heavy job and chooses the time of comparison among several checkpoints with geometrically decreasing probabilities, so that the algorithm has to leave either the heavy job or its earlier backlog unfinished. Averaging concatenates independent copies at decreasing time scales and uses martingale concentration to make the backlog of the algorithm almost deterministic, which allows us to amplify again.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

We consider one of the most basic problems in online scheduling: jobs arrive over time at a single machine, and the goal is to minimize their total weighted flow time. Each job~$j$ has a release time~$r_j$, a size~$p_j$, and a weight~$w_j$, which become known at time~$r_j$. The machine processes at most one job at a time, and a job may be preempted and resumed later. If job~$j$ completes at time~$C_j$, its flow time $C_j-r_j$ is the time that it spends in the system, and the objective is to minimize $\sum_j w_j(C_j-r_j)$. An online algorithm decides at every time which job to process, without knowledge of future jobs. It is \emph{$R$-competitive} if, on every instance, its (expected) cost is at most $R$ times the optimal cost, up to an additive constant.

Without weights, the problem is easy even online: always processing the job with the shortest remaining processing time is optimal~\cite{Schrage68}. With weights, it is considerably harder. Already the offline problem is strongly NP-hard~\cite{LabetoulleLLR84}, and a polynomial-time approximation scheme was found only recently~\cite{ArmbrusterRW23}, after a long line of work~\cite{ChekuriK02,BansalP10,BatraGK18,FeigeKL19,RohwedderW21}. In the online setting, the first algorithm with a nontrivial competitive ratio is due to Chekuri, Khanna, and Zhu~\cite{ChekuriKZ01}. It is $O(\log^2P)$-competitive, where $P$ is the ratio between the largest and the smallest job size, and it needs to know~$P$ in advance. Bansal and Dhamdhere~\cite{BansalD07} gave an $O(\log W)$-competitive algorithm, where $W$ is the ratio between the largest and the smallest weight. Azar and Touitou~\cite{AzarT18} gave an $O(\log P)$-competitive and an $O(\log D)$-competitive algorithm, where $D$ is the ratio between the largest and the smallest density $w_j/p_j$, and combined these results with the algorithm of Bansal and Dhamdhere into an $O(\log\min\{P,D,W\})$-competitive algorithm. With a slightly faster machine, a constant competitive ratio is possible: on a machine that is faster by a factor of $1+\eps$, the simple Highest Density First rule is $O(1/\eps)$-competitive~\cite{BecchettiLMP06}.

It was widely believed that weighted flow time admits an $O(1)$-competitive online algorithm, until Bansal and Chan~\cite{BansalC09} showed that every deterministic online algorithm has competitive ratio
\[
  \Omega\biggl(\min\biggl\{\sqrt{\frac{\log W}{\log\log W}},\,\sqrt{\frac{\log\log P}{\log\log\log P}}\biggr\}\biggr).
\]
Their proof starts from the trivial lower bound of one and improves it step by step. As usual for lower bounds against deterministic algorithms, their adversary is adaptive: it chooses the next jobs depending on the decisions that the algorithm has made so far. Against a randomized algorithm, however, an oblivious adversary has to fix the instance in advance, without knowing the random choices of the algorithm, so this construction does not apply.

Randomization can be very powerful for flow time. For instance, if job sizes are unknown until the jobs complete, every deterministic algorithm for unweighted flow time on a single machine has competitive ratio $\Omega(n^{1/3})$, where $n$ is the number of jobs~\cite{MotwaniPT94}, while a randomized version of the multilevel feedback algorithm~\cite{KalyanasundaramP03} is $O(\log n)$-competitive~\cite{BecchettiL04}, which is best possible~\cite{MotwaniPT94}. For weighted flow time, however, Chekuri, Khanna, and Zhu~\cite{ChekuriKZ01} showed that no randomized online algorithm is $O(1)$-competitive on $m\ge2$ identical machines, by a lower bound of $\Omega(\min\{\sqrt P,\sqrt W,(n/m)^{1/4}\})$. On a single machine, the only lower bound for randomized algorithms that we are aware of is the constant~$4/3$, also due to Chekuri, Khanna, and Zhu. Thus it has remained open whether a randomized online algorithm can be $O(1)$-competitive for weighted flow time on a single machine.

\paragraph{Our results.}
We show that this is not the case.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
No randomized online algorithm for minimizing the total weighted flow time on a single machine is $O(1)$-competitive against an oblivious adversary. More precisely, for all $R\ge1$ and $\gamma\ge0$, there is a probability distribution~$\cD$ over finitely many instances such that every deterministic online algorithm~$A$ satisfies
\[
  \E_{I\sim\cD}\bigl[F_A(I)\bigr]\ge R\cdot\E_{I\sim\cD}\bigl[\OPT(I)\bigr]+\gamma.
\]
\end{theorem}

Here $F_A(I)$ denotes the weighted flow time of the schedule that $A$ produces on the instance~$I$, and $\OPT(I)$ is the optimal weighted flow time of~$I$. By Yao's principle, the second statement implies the first, and the term~$\gamma$ rules out additive constants. The lower bound holds in a strong form: the algorithm learns the size and the weight of each job at its release, it may process several jobs simultaneously at fractional rates, and it may know the distribution~$\cD$ in advance. In particular, it may know upper bounds on~$W$ and~$P$, as well as the number of jobs, which is the same for all instances in the support of~$\cD$.

Thus, even with randomization, the competitive ratio has to grow with the parameters of the instance. Our construction gives an explicit, if weak, dependence on~$W$, $P$, and the number~$n$ of jobs.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:quant}
For all $W,P,n\ge16$, every randomized online algorithm for weighted flow time has competitive ratio
\[
  \Omega\bigl(\min\{\log\log W,\,\log\log\log P,\,\log\log\log n\}\bigr)
\]
against an oblivious adversary on instances with at most~$n$ jobs in which the ratios between the largest and the smallest weight and size are at most~$W$ and~$P$, respectively.
\end{theorem}

In particular, for $W=P=n$, the competitive ratio on instances with at most~$n$ jobs is $\Omega(\log\log\log n)$. The dependence on~$W$ and~$P$ is exponentially weaker than in the deterministic lower bound of Bansal and Chan, because, to reach the lower bound~$R$, each step of our construction improves the bound only by a factor of $1+2^{-\Theta(R)}$; see \cref{sec:conclusion}, where we also discuss the dependence on~$n$.

\subsection{Technical overview}\label{sec:overview}

By Yao's principle, it suffices to construct, for every~$R$, one distribution over instances on which every deterministic algorithm is worse than $R$ times the optimum in expectation. The difficulty compared with deterministic lower bounds is that the instance cannot react to the algorithm: the adversary does not know which jobs the algorithm has left unfinished. Instead, our distributions hide from the algorithm the time at which its unfinished weight is compared with that of an optimal schedule.

\paragraph{Unfinished weight at a hidden time.}
The weighted flow time of a schedule is the integral over time of its \emph{unfinished weight}, the total weight of the jobs that have been released but not yet completed. Hence an online algorithm is $c$-competitive if it is \emph{locally $c$-competitive}, that is, if its unfinished weight is at every time at most $c$ times the minimum weight that a schedule can leave unfinished at this time. Conversely, Becchetti et al.~\cite{BecchettiLMP06} showed that every $c$-competitive deterministic online algorithm is also $c$-competitive for minimizing the weight of the jobs that are unfinished at an unknown deadline. Our lower bound follows this converse direction: at a time that it cannot foresee, every algorithm leaves much more weight unfinished than necessary, and a stream of small jobs makes this gap persist long enough to dominate the weighted flow time.

\paragraph{Hard terminal distributions.}
The building blocks of our construction are \emph{terminal distributions}: distributions over pairs $(I,T)$ of an instance~$I$ and a \emph{terminal time}~$T$ after all release times of~$I$. The algorithm processes~$I$ without knowing~$T$, and at time~$T$ we compare two quantities. On the offline side, $U^*(I,T)$ is the minimum weight that a schedule of~$I$ can leave unfinished at time~$T$. On the online side, we count only the unfinished jobs with remaining size at least~$1$, which form the \emph{robust backlog} of the algorithm; the algorithm cannot complete any of them before time~$T+1$. We cap the weight of the robust backlog at a parameter~$M$, for a reason that we explain with amplification below. A terminal distribution is \emph{$(c,M)$-hard} if $\E[U^*(I,T)]\le1$, while every deterministic algorithm has an expected capped robust backlog of at least~$c$ (\cref{def:hard}). We call~$c$ the \emph{gap} and~$M$ the \emph{cap}. In words, at the hidden terminal time, the algorithm leaves in expectation $c$ times as much weight unfinished as necessary, in jobs that it cannot complete quickly.

\paragraph{Flooding.}
A $(2R,M)$-hard terminal distribution yields the lower bound~$R$ (\cref{lem:flooding}). From the terminal time~$T$ on, we release a long stream of small jobs: every $d=1/(2M)$ time units, a job of size~$d$ and weight~$1/2$ arrives. The stream alone keeps the machine busy, and its jobs have density~$M$. Hence, once the algorithm has spent $y$ units of time on earlier jobs after time~$T$, stream jobs of total weight more than~$My$ are unfinished. As long as $y<1$, no job of its robust backlog can be completed, and once $y\ge1$, the unfinished stream jobs weigh more than~$M$. Either way, the unfinished weight of the algorithm never drops below its capped robust backlog while the stream lasts (\cref{fig:flooding}). An offline schedule, in contrast, can leave weight $U^*(I,T)$ unfinished at time~$T$ and then process every stream job right after its release, which keeps its unfinished weight at $U^*(I,T)+1/2$. For a long stream, the expected costs therefore differ by a factor of almost $2R/(1+1/2)>R$.

It thus suffices to construct $(c,M)$-hard terminal distributions with arbitrarily large gap~$c$. We start from the trivial $(1,M)$-hard distribution that consists of a single job of size~$2$ and weight~$1$ with terminal time~$1$, and we alternate two operations, amplification and averaging. Both build on the following way of combining copies of a hard distribution.

\paragraph{Copies at decreasing time scales.}
Given independent samples $(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_m,T_m)$ of a hard terminal distribution, we release \emph{copy~$i$}, the instance~$I_i$ stretched in time and size by a factor~$s_i$, right after the terminal time of copy~$i-1$. The factor~$s_i$ is so large that all later copies together are shorter than~$s_i/2$ and have total size less than~$s_i/2$ (\cref{fig:amplification}). This construction has two consequences. First, as copy~$i$ is stretched by~$s_i$, every job in its robust backlog has remaining size at least~$s_i$ at its terminal time, so more than~$s_i/2$ remains even if the algorithm spends the entire duration of the later copies on this job. Second, given the earlier copies, copy~$i$ is a fresh sample of the hard distribution, and the later copies arrive only from its terminal time on (\cref{fig:copies}). Hence the algorithm acts on copy~$i$ like a deterministic algorithm on a fresh sample, and the capped robust backlog~$X_i$ of copy~$i$ has conditional expectation at least~$c$ given the earlier copies, no matter how the algorithm adapts to them. An offline schedule can process each copy in its own time window and thereby leave expected weight at most~$1$ per copy unfinished (\cref{lem:copies}).

\paragraph{Amplification.}
Amplification takes $k=2M+1$ copies of a $(c,M)$-hard distribution and, right after them, releases a heavy job~$J$ of weight~$2M$ whose size~$V$ is at least the total size of all copies. The terminal time is one of $k$ checkpoints $h_k<h_{k-1}<\dots<h_1$ after the release of~$J$, where checkpoint~$h_i$ leaves just enough time to complete~$J$ and copies $i+1,\dots,k$. We choose the checkpoint~$h_K$ at random, with $\Pr[K\ge i]=2^{1-i}$ for $i=1,\dots,k$. For the terminal time~$h_i$, an offline schedule can complete~$J$ and copies $i+1,\dots,k$, which leaves expected weight at most~$i$ unfinished. For the earliest checkpoint~$h_k$, it can instead complete all copies and leave only~$J$, of weight $k-1$, unfinished. Hence the minimum unfinished weight at the terminal time is at most $\E[K]-\Pr[K=k]$ in expectation.

The algorithm, which does not know~$K$, faces a dilemma at every checkpoint~$h_i$: either~$J$ belongs to its robust backlog, or the algorithm has spent almost all the time since the release of~$J$ on~$J$, and then the robust backlogs of copies $1,\dots,i$ are still there. Let~$h_\ell$ be the latest checkpoint at which~$J$ still has remaining size at least~$1$. Since remaining sizes do not increase over time, $J$ has remaining size at least~$1$ exactly at the checkpoints $h_\ell,\dots,h_k$. At the terminal time~$h_K$, the robust backlog of the algorithm thus contains~$J$ if $K\ge\ell$, and the robust backlogs of all copies $j\le K$ with $j<\ell$. Compared with keeping the backlogs $X_1,\dots,X_K$ of all copies $j\le K$, the algorithm may have cleared those of copies $\ell,\dots,K$, but~$J$ compensates for them: given $K\ge\ell$, the expected number of these copies is at most two, as~$K$ is geometric, and each~$X_j$ is at most~$M$, because of the cap (\cref{fig:amp-accounting}). Thus the robust backlog of the algorithm has expected weight at least $c\,\E[K]$. After normalizing the weights, the gap improves by the factor $\E[K]/(\E[K]-\Pr[K=k])=1+\eta$, where $\eta=1/(2^k-2)$ (\cref{lem:amplification}).

\paragraph{Averaging.}
Amplification turns a $(c,M)$-hard distribution into a $((1+\eta)c,(k+1)M)$-hard one: the gap improves, but the cap grows. Since~$\eta$ decreases exponentially with the cap, we have to bring the cap down before we amplify again. To this end, averaging takes a $(c,M)$-hard distribution, in our construction the output of amplification, concatenates $m$ copies of it, takes the end of the last copy as the terminal time, and divides all weights by~$m$. The capped robust backlogs $X_1,\dots,X_m$ of the copies are not independent, because the algorithm adapts to the past, but they lie in $[0,M]$ and have conditional expectations at least~$c$. A second-moment bound for the associated martingale shows that their average, capped at~$c$, still has expectation at least $c-\eps$, so the averaged distribution is $(c-\eps,c)$-hard (\cref{lem:averaging}). In other words, averaging makes the guarantee almost deterministic, which allows us to reduce the cap to the gap.

\paragraph{Putting things together.}
We fix the cap $M=\lceil4R\rceil$ and alternate amplification and averaging. Each round multiplies the gap by $1+\eta/2$ and keeps the cap at~$M$, so after $2^{O(R)}$ rounds the gap exceeds~$2R$ (\cref{thm:hard}). Each round combines $2^{O(R)}$ copies, which leads to the bounds on~$W$, $P$, and~$n$ in \cref{thm:quant}.

\paragraph{Comparison with the deterministic lower bound.}
Our construction builds on the deterministic lower bound of Bansal and Chan~\cite{BansalC09}, who introduced the idea of improving a lower bound step by step. Several of our ingredients appear there in adaptive form: Bansal and Chan measure the unfinished weight of the algorithm by jobs with large remaining size, run subinstances one after another at geometrically decreasing scales, release a final heavy job, and turn unfinished weight into flow time by a stream of small jobs. Their adversary, however, observes the algorithm: each subinstance ends when the algorithm has fallen behind, and how the instance continues after the heavy job depends on how the algorithm processes it. The new ingredients of our construction are the hidden random terminal time, which makes the amplification step oblivious, the analysis of copies against algorithms that adapt to earlier copies (\cref{lem:copies}), and the cap together with averaging, which make amplification repeatable although its guarantee holds only in expectation.

\subsection{Further related work}

Without weights, randomization does not help on identical parallel machines: the Shortest Remaining Processing Time rule is $O(\log\min\{P,n/m\})$-competitive on $m$ machines, which is best possible for $m\ge2$, also for randomized algorithms~\cite{LeonardiR07}. Related models include weighted flow time with speed augmentation~\cite{BecchettiLMP06,AnandGK12} and flow time with uncertain job sizes~\cite{AzarLT21,AzarLT22}.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec:prelim}

\paragraph{Instances and schedules.}
An \emph{instance}~$I$ is a finite set of jobs; each job $j\in I$ has a release time $r_j\ge0$, a size $p_j>0$, and a weight $w_j>0$. We identify a job with the triple $(r_j,p_j,w_j)$, so an algorithm knows nothing about a job but these parameters. For a set~$E$ of jobs, we write $w(E)=\sum_{j\in E}w_j$ and $p(E)=\sum_{j\in E}p_j$. A \emph{schedule} of~$I$ assigns to each job $j\in I$ a measurable rate function $x_j\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,1]$ such that $x_j(t)=0$ for $t<r_j$, $\sum_{j\in I}x_j(t)\le1$ for all~$t$, and $\int_0^\infty x_j(t)\dd t\le p_j$. The \emph{remaining size} of~$j$ at time~$t$ is $p_j-\int_0^t x_j(t')\dd t'$; it is continuous and non-increasing in~$t$. Job~$j$ \emph{completes} at the first time~$C_j$ at which its remaining size is zero ($C_j=\infty$ if there is no such time), and it is \emph{unfinished} at time~$t$ if $r_j\le t<C_j$. The weighted flow time of the schedule is
\[
  \sum_{j\in I}w_j(C_j-r_j)=\int_0^\infty U(t)\dd t,
\]
where the \emph{unfinished weight} $U(t)$ is the total weight of the jobs that are unfinished at time~$t$. We denote by $\OPT(I)$ the infimum of the weighted flow time over all schedules of~$I$.

Schedules with rates in $\{0,1\}$ are the usual preemptive schedules. Allowing fractional rates does not change the optimum: the preemptive Earliest Deadline First rule, with the completion times of a given schedule as deadlines, completes every job no later than this schedule. For online algorithms, fractional rates only strengthen our lower bounds.

\paragraph{Online algorithms.}
A \emph{deterministic online algorithm}~$A$ maps every instance~$I$ to a schedule $A(I)$ of~$I$ subject to the following non-anticipation condition: for every $t\ge0$ and all instances $I$ and $I'$ that contain the same jobs with release times before~$t$, the schedules $A(I)$ and $A(I')$ assign the same rates to these jobs during $[0,t)$. In other words, the decisions of~$A$ up to time~$t$ depend only on the jobs released before~$t$, including their sizes and weights. In particular, these jobs have the same remaining sizes at time~$t$ in $A(I)$ and in $A(I')$. We write $F_A(I)$ for the weighted flow time of $A(I)$. A deterministic online algorithm may be designed for a specific input distribution; for example, it may use the number of jobs if this number is the same for all instances in the support of the distribution. It is nevertheless defined on all instances, which matters because the proofs of \cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:scaling} and \cref{lem:copies} run it on auxiliary instances.

A \emph{randomized online algorithm}~$\cA$ is a probability distribution over deterministic online algorithms such that $F_A(I)$ is a random variable for every instance~$I$ when $A\sim\cA$. It is \emph{$R$-competitive} (against an oblivious adversary) if there is a constant~$\Gamma$ such that $\E_{A\sim\cA}[F_A(I)]\le R\cdot\OPT(I)+\Gamma$ for every instance~$I$. We use the easy direction of Yao's principle~\cite{Yao77}; see also~\cite{BorodinE98}.

\begin{lemma}[Yao's principle]\label{lem:yao}
Let $\cD$ be a probability distribution over finitely many instances, and let $R>0$ and $\gamma\in\RR$ be such that every deterministic online algorithm~$A$ satisfies $\E_{I\sim\cD}[F_A(I)]\ge R\cdot\E_{I\sim\cD}[\OPT(I)]+\gamma$. Then for every randomized online algorithm~$\cA$, there is an instance~$I$ in the support of~$\cD$ with $\E_{A\sim\cA}[F_A(I)]\ge R\cdot\OPT(I)+\gamma$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $\E_{A\sim\cA}[F_A(I)]=\infty$ for some~$I$ in the support of~$\cD$, this instance satisfies the claim. Otherwise, taking the expectation of the assumption over $A\sim\cA$ and using linearity of expectation over the finitely many instances in the support of~$\cD$, we obtain
\[
  \E_{I\sim\cD}\bigl[\E_{A\sim\cA}[F_A(I)]-R\cdot\OPT(I)\bigr]\ge\gamma.
\]
Hence some instance in the support of~$\cD$ satisfies the claim.
\end{proof}

\section{Hard terminal distributions}\label{sec:hard}

In this section, we introduce the notion of hardness that underlies our construction, show that it implies lower bounds for weighted flow time, and derive \cref{thm:main} from the existence of hard terminal distributions, which we prove in \cref{sec:construction}.

\paragraph{Terminal distributions.}
A \emph{terminal instance} is a pair $(I,T)$ of an instance~$I$ and a \emph{terminal time} $T>0$ such that all jobs of~$I$ are released before~$T$. A \emph{terminal distribution} is a probability distribution over finitely many terminal instances. An online algorithm processes only the instance~$I$; in particular, the schedule $A(I)$ does not depend on~$T$. In general, $I$ and~$T$ are correlated, so the algorithm may learn something about~$T$ from the jobs it sees, and it may know the distribution in advance.

For a terminal instance $(I,T)$, let $U^*(I,T)$ be the minimum total weight of the jobs that a schedule of~$I$ leaves unfinished at time~$T$; the minimum exists because there are only finitely many sets of jobs. For a deterministic online algorithm~$A$, the \emph{robust backlog} of~$A$ at time~$T$ is the set of jobs of~$I$ whose remaining size in $A(I)$ at time~$T$ is at least~$1$. These jobs are unfinished at time~$T$, and none of them can be completed before time~$T+1$. For $M>0$, the \emph{capped robust backlog} is
\[
  \Phi_M(A;I,T)=\min\bigl\{M,\,w(\text{robust backlog of $A$ at time $T$})\bigr\}.
\]

\begin{definition}\label{def:hard}
Let $0<c\le M$. A terminal distribution~$\cD$ is \emph{$(c,M)$-hard} if
\[
  \E\bigl[U^*(I,T)\bigr]\le1
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \E\bigl[\Phi_M(A;I,T)\bigr]\ge c\quad\text{for every deterministic online algorithm~$A$,}
\]
where the expectations are over $(I,T)\sim\cD$. We call~$c$ the \emph{gap} and~$M$ the \emph{cap}.
\end{definition}

The cap is needed for amplification (\cref{sec:amplification}), where a heavy job compensates for the robust backlogs of copies that the algorithm may have cleared; this works because the cap limits the contribution of each copy to~$M$. The following observation provides the starting point of our construction and two rules that we use repeatedly.

\begin{observation}\label{obs:basic}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item\label{obs:base} The terminal distribution that consists of a single job with release time~$0$, size~$2$, and weight~$1$, together with the terminal time~$1$, is $(1,M)$-hard for every $M\ge1$.
\item\label{obs:monotone} If~$\cD$ is $(c,M)$-hard, then~$\cD$ is $(c',M')$-hard for all $0<c'\le c$ and $M'\ge M$.
\item\label{obs:scaling} Let $\lambda>0$ and $0<c\le M$, and let~$\cD$ be a terminal distribution such that $\E[U^*(I,T)]\le\lambda$ and $\E[\Phi_{\lambda M}(A;I,T)]\ge\lambda c$ for every deterministic online algorithm~$A$, where the expectations are over $(I,T)\sim\cD$. Then the terminal distribution obtained from~$\cD$ by dividing all weights by~$\lambda$ is $(c,M)$-hard.
\end{enumerate}
\end{observation}

\begin{proof}
\ref{obs:base}~No schedule completes the job by time~$1$, and in every schedule its remaining size at time~$1$ is at least~$1$. Hence $U^*=1$ and $\Phi_M=\min\{M,1\}=1$.

\ref{obs:monotone}~This follows from $\Phi_{M'}\ge\Phi_M$.

\ref{obs:scaling}~For an instance~$I$, let $I/\lambda$ denote the instance with all weights divided by~$\lambda$. Since $U^*(I/\lambda,T)=U^*(I,T)/\lambda$, we have $\E[U^*(I/\lambda,T)]\le1$, where here and below the expectations are over $(I,T)\sim\cD$. Let~$A$ be a deterministic online algorithm. As~$A$ need not treat~$I$ and $I/\lambda$ alike, we apply the assumption to the algorithm~$A_\lambda$ that on input~$I$ runs~$A$ on $I/\lambda$ and uses the same rates, that is, $A_\lambda(I)=A(I/\lambda)$. Since $I$ and $I/\lambda$ have the same release times, $A_\lambda$ satisfies the non-anticipation condition. The robust backlogs of~$A$ on $(I/\lambda,T)$ and of~$A_\lambda$ on $(I,T)$ consist of the same jobs up to the scaling of their weights, so $\Phi_M(A;I/\lambda,T)=\Phi_{\lambda M}(A_\lambda;I,T)/\lambda$. By assumption, the right-hand side has expectation at least~$c$.
\end{proof}

We need a version for terminal distributions in which the stream does not depend on the algorithm.

\begin{lemma}[Flooding]\label{lem:flooding}
Let $R\ge1$, and let~$\cD$ be a $(2R,M)$-hard terminal distribution. Then for every $\gamma\ge0$, there is a probability distribution~$\hat\cD$ over finitely many instances such that every deterministic online algorithm~$A$ satisfies
\[
  \E_{\hat I\sim\hat\cD}\bigl[F_A(\hat I)\bigr]\ge R\cdot\E_{\hat I\sim\hat\cD}\bigl[\OPT(\hat I)\bigr]+\gamma.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $H\ge1$ and $Q>0$ be such that $T\le H$, $p(I)\le H$, and $w(I)\le Q$ for every terminal instance $(I,T)$ in the support of~$\cD$. Let $d=1/(2M)$, and let the length~$L$ of the stream be a positive multiple of~$d$, which we choose at the end of the proof. Given $(I,T)\sim\cD$, the instance~$\hat I$ consists of the jobs of~$I$, which we call \emph{old}, and of $L/d$ \emph{stream} jobs of size~$d$ and weight~$1/2$, released at times $T,T+d,\dots,T+L-d$. Let~$\hat\cD$ be the distribution of~$\hat I$. Since~$\hat I$ is determined by $(I,T)$, all expectations in this proof are over $(I,T)\sim\cD$; for functions of~$\hat I$, they agree with expectations over $\hat I\sim\hat\cD$.

\emph{Online cost.} Fix a deterministic online algorithm~$A$ and a terminal instance $(I,T)$ in the support of~$\cD$. As all stream jobs are released at time~$T$ or later, $A(\hat I)$ and $A(I)$ assign the same rates to the old jobs during $[0,T)$, so the old jobs with remaining size at least~$1$ at time~$T$ in $A(\hat I)$ form the robust backlog of~$A$ on $(I,T)$. Let $\Phi=\Phi_M(A;I,T)\le M$. We show that the unfinished weight of $A(\hat I)$ is at least~$\Phi$ throughout $[T,T+L)$. For $0\le\theta<L$, let~$y$ be the total processing that the old jobs receive during $[T,T+\theta)$ in $A(\hat I)$. If $y<1$, then all jobs of the robust backlog are still unfinished at time $T+\theta$, and their total weight is at least~$\Phi$. If $y\ge1$, consider the stream jobs released by time $T+\theta$. Their total size $d\,(\lfloor\theta/d\rfloor+1)$ exceeds~$\theta$, and they received at most $\theta-y$ processing, so their total remaining size exceeds~$y$. As each stream job has size~$d$, more than $y/d$ of them are unfinished at time~$T+\theta$, and their total weight exceeds $y/(2d)=My\ge M\ge\Phi$. In both cases, the total weight of the jobs unfinished at time $T+\theta$ is at least~$\Phi$; see \cref{fig:flooding}. Integrating over $\theta\in[0,L)$ yields $F_A(\hat I)\ge L\cdot\Phi$, and by the hardness of~$\cD$,
\[
  \E\bigl[F_A(\hat I)\bigr]\ge L\cdot\E[\Phi]\ge 2RL.
\]

\emph{Optimal cost.} Consider the following schedule of~$\hat I$. Until time~$T$, follow a schedule of~$I$ that leaves weight $U^*(I,T)$ unfinished at time~$T$. During $[T,T+L)$, process each stream job in the $d$ time units after its release. After time $T+L$, complete the remaining old jobs. The old jobs contribute at most~$QH$ to the weighted flow time before time~$T$, exactly $U^*(I,T)\cdot L$ during $[T,T+L)$, and at most~$QH$ afterwards, since their total remaining size is at most~$H$. The stream jobs contribute $(L/d)\cdot d/2=L/2$. Hence $\OPT(\hat I)\le 2QH+(U^*(I,T)+1/2)\,L$, and therefore
\[
  \E\bigl[\OPT(\hat I)\bigr]\le 2QH+\tfrac32L.
\]

Combining both bounds, we obtain $\E[F_A(\hat I)]-R\cdot\E[\OPT(\hat I)]\ge R\,(L/2-2QH)$, which is at least~$\gamma$ if $L\ge4QH+2\gamma/R$. We choose such an~$L$; since it does not depend on~$A$, this proves the lemma.
\end{proof}

The main technical result is the existence of hard terminal distributions with arbitrarily large gaps.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:hard}
For every $R\ge1$, there is a $(2R,M)$-hard terminal distribution with $M=\lceil4R\rceil$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:main}]
Let $R\ge1$ and $\gamma\ge0$. By \cref{thm:hard} and \cref{lem:flooding}, there is a distribution~$\hat\cD$ over finitely many instances such that every deterministic online algorithm~$A$ satisfies $\E_{\hat I\sim\hat\cD}[F_A(\hat I)]\ge R\cdot\E_{\hat I\sim\hat\cD}[\OPT(\hat I)]+\gamma$. This is the second statement. For the first one, let~$\cA$ be a randomized online algorithm and $\Gamma\ge0$. Applying \cref{lem:yao} to the distribution~$\hat\cD$ obtained for $\gamma=\Gamma+1$ yields an instance~$\hat I$ with $\E_{A\sim\cA}[F_A(\hat I)]\ge R\cdot\OPT(\hat I)+\Gamma+1$, so~$\cA$ is not $R$-competitive with additive constant~$\Gamma$. Since $R$ and~$\Gamma$ are arbitrary, no randomized online algorithm is $O(1)$-competitive.
\end{proof}

\section{Constructing hard terminal distributions}\label{sec:construction}

We prove \cref{thm:hard} by alternating two operations on hard terminal distributions. Amplification (\cref{sec:amplification}) multiplies the gap by $1+\eta$ for a small $\eta>0$ that depends on the cap, but it increases the cap. Averaging (\cref{sec:averaging}) reduces the cap to the gap while losing only a little in the gap. Both operations concatenate independent copies of a terminal distribution at decreasing time scales, which we analyze first.

\subsection{Copies at decreasing time scales}\label{sec:copies}

We define the concatenation of copies and show that an offline schedule can handle the copies separately, whereas an online algorithm faces each copy as a fresh sample of the distribution.

Let~$\cD$ be a terminal distribution. A \emph{bound} for~$\cD$ is a number $H\ge1$ such that $T\le H$ and $p(I)\le H$ for every $(I,T)$ in the support of~$\cD$. Fix a bound~$H$ for~$\cD$ and a positive integer~$m$, and write $[m]=\{1,\dots,m\}$. We set $\rho=2H+1$, $S_i=\rho^{m-i}-1$ for $i\in\{0,\dots,m\}$, $s_i=2\rho^{m-i}$ for $i\in[m]$, and $V=S_0=\rho^m-1$. These numbers satisfy, for every $i\in[m]$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scales}
  S_i=H\sum_{j=i+1}^m s_j
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  s_i-S_i=S_i+2,
\end{equation}
where the first identity is a geometric sum; it also holds for $i=0$. We draw $(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_m,T_m)$ independently from~$\cD$, and we let $u_0=0$ and $u_i=u_{i-1}+s_iT_i$ for $i\in[m]$. For every $i\in[m]$, \emph{copy~$i$} contains, for each job $j\in I_i$, a job with release time $u_{i-1}+s_ir_j$, size $s_ip_j$, and weight~$w_j$, which we call the copy of~$j$. Thus copy~$i$ is the instance~$I_i$, stretched in time and size by the factor~$s_i$ and started at time~$u_{i-1}$; its terminal time is~$u_i$. The \emph{concatenation}~$\bar I$ is the union of copies $1,\dots,m$, and $\tau=u_m$ is its \emph{end}. The concatenation depends on~$H$, which we suppress in the notation; all statements below hold for every bound~$H$ for~$\cD$.

The scales ensure that all copies after copy~$i$ fit into a time interval of length~$S_i$, while the jobs in the robust backlog of copy~$i$ survive them with remaining size at least $S_i+2$; see \cref{fig:copies}. Subinstances at geometrically decreasing scales that run one after another are also a key element of the construction of Bansal and Chan~\cite{BansalC09}. There, each subinstance starts as soon as the algorithm has fallen behind in the previous one; here, copy~$i+1$ starts at the terminal time of copy~$i$, which is random and hidden from the algorithm.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:copies}
Let~$\cD$ be a $(c,M)$-hard terminal distribution, and let~$\bar I$ be the concatenation of $m$ copies of~$\cD$ with end~$\tau$, as above.
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item\label{it:geometry} The jobs of copy~$i$ are released during $[u_{i-1},u_i)$. For every $i\in\{0,\dots,m\}$, copies $i+1,\dots,m$ have total size at most~$S_i$, and $\tau-u_i\le S_i$. In particular, $p(\bar I)\le V$ and $\tau\le V$.
\item\label{it:offline} There is a schedule of~$\bar I$ in which, for every $i\in[m]$, the jobs of copy~$i$ that are unfinished at time~$\tau$ have total weight $B_i=U^*(I_i,T_i)$. Moreover, $\E[B_i]\le1$.
\item\label{it:online} Let~$A$ be a deterministic online algorithm, and let~$\tilde I$ be an instance that consists of~$\bar I$ and possibly further jobs released at time~$\tau$ or later; these jobs may depend arbitrarily on $(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_m,T_m)$. For $i\in[m]$, let~$E_i$ be the set of jobs of copy~$i$ whose remaining size at time~$u_i$ in $A(\tilde I)$ is at least~$s_i$, and let $X_i=\min\{M,w(E_i)\}$. Then $X_i$ is determined by $(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_i,T_i)$, and
\[
  \E\bigl[X_i\bigm|(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_{i-1},T_{i-1})\bigr]\ge c.
\]
Moreover, every job of~$E_i$ has remaining size at least $S_i+2$ at time~$\tau$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

In part~\ref{it:online}, the set~$E_i$ is the robust backlog of copy~$i$ measured in the units of copy~$i$: after shrinking time and sizes by the factor~$s_i$, its jobs have remaining size at least~$1$ at the terminal time of copy~$i$.

\begin{proof}
\ref{it:geometry}~A job of copy~$i$ is released at time $u_{i-1}+s_ir_j$ with $0\le r_j<T_i$, hence during $[u_{i-1},u_i)$. Copy~$i'$ has total size $s_{i'}\,p(I_{i'})\le s_{i'}H$, and it is released during the interval $[u_{i'-1},u_{i'})$ of length $s_{i'}T_{i'}\le s_{i'}H$. Summing over $i'>i$ and using~\eqref{eq:scales} proves the second claim; the case $i=0$ gives the third.

\ref{it:offline}~For $i\in[m]$, fix a schedule of~$I_i$ that leaves weight $U^*(I_i,T_i)$ unfinished at time~$T_i$, with rate functions~$x_j$. During $[u_{i-1},u_i)$, process the copy of each $j\in I_i$ at rate $x_j((t-u_{i-1})/s_i)$ at time~$t$. This stretched schedule processes the copy of~$j$ only after its release, and the copy is unfinished at time~$u_i$ if and only if~$j$ is unfinished at time~$T_i$. Since the intervals $[u_{i-1},u_i)$ are disjoint, these schedules combine into a schedule of~$\bar I$, which does not process copy~$i$ after time~$u_i$. Moreover, $\E[B_i]=\E_{(I,T)\sim\cD}[U^*(I,T)]\le1$.

\ref{it:online}~Fix $i\in[m]$ and condition on $(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_{i-1},T_{i-1})$; since~$\cD$ has finite support, we condition on an event of positive probability. Then copies $1,\dots,i-1$ and the time~$u_{i-1}$ are fixed. We show that, from the point of view of copy~$i$, the algorithm~$A$ behaves like a deterministic online algorithm~$A_i$ for~$\cD$.

\emph{The algorithm~$A_i$.} For an arbitrary instance~$I$, let $\sigma_i(I)$ be the instance obtained from~$I$ in the same way as copy~$i$ is obtained from~$I_i$, that is, by stretching~$I$ by the factor~$s_i$ and starting it at time~$u_{i-1}$, and let $\bar I_i(I)$ be the union of copies $1,\dots,i-1$ and $\sigma_i(I)$. On input~$I$, the algorithm~$A_i$ runs~$A$ on $\bar I_i(I)$ and processes each job $j\in I$ at time~$t$ at the rate at which~$A$ processes the copy of~$j$ at time $u_{i-1}+s_it$. This is a schedule of~$I$: the rates sum to at most~$1$, job~$j$ is not processed before~$r_j$ because its copy is not processed before $u_{i-1}+s_ir_j$, and job~$j$ receives at most~$p_j$ processing because its copy receives at most~$s_ip_j$. The algorithm~$A_i$ satisfies the non-anticipation condition, because the decisions of~$A$ before time $u_{i-1}+s_it$ depend only on copies $1,\dots,i-1$ and on the copies of the jobs of~$I$ released before~$t$. Since time is stretched by the factor~$s_i$, the remaining size of~$j$ at time~$t$ in $A_i(I)$ equals $1/s_i$ times the remaining size of the copy of~$j$ at time $u_{i-1}+s_it$ in $A(\bar I_i(I))$.

\emph{Agreement before~$u_i$.} The instances $\bar I_i(I_i)$ and~$\tilde I$ contain the same jobs with release times before~$u_i$: copies $1,\dots,i$ are released before~$u_i$ by~\ref{it:geometry}, while copies $i+1,\dots,m$ and all further jobs of~$\tilde I$ are released at time~$u_i$ or later. By the non-anticipation condition, $A(\bar I_i(I_i))$ and $A(\tilde I)$ process copies $1,\dots,i$ in the same way during $[0,u_i)$. In particular, $X_i$ is determined by $(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_i,T_i)$. Moreover, as $u_i=u_{i-1}+s_iT_i$, a job of copy~$i$ belongs to~$E_i$ if and only if its original has remaining size at least~$1$ at time~$T_i$ in $A_i(I_i)$. Hence
\[
  X_i=\Phi_M(A_i;I_i,T_i).
\]
The algorithm~$A_i$ depends only on the event we condition on, while $(I_i,T_i)$ is independent of this event and distributed according to~$\cD$. Since~$\cD$ is $(c,M)$-hard, the conditional expectation of~$X_i$ is at least~$c$.

\emph{Survival.} A job of~$E_i$ has remaining size at least~$s_i$ at time~$u_i$ and receives at most $\tau-u_i\le S_i$ processing during $[u_i,\tau)$. By~\eqref{eq:scales}, its remaining size at time~$\tau$ is at least $s_i-S_i=S_i+2$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Amplification}\label{sec:amplification}

Amplification appends a heavy job to $k$ copies of a terminal distribution and hides the terminal time among $k$ checkpoints after the release of this job. Let $M\ge1$ be an integer and $k=2M+1$. Let~$\cD$ be a terminal distribution with a bound~$H$, and let~$\bar I$ be the concatenation of~$k$ copies of~$\cD$, with the notation of \cref{sec:copies} for $m=k$. We add a job~$J$ with release time~$\tau$, size~$V$, and weight~$2M$, and we define the \emph{checkpoints}
\[
  h_i=\tau+V+S_i\qquad(i\in[k]).
\]
Since $S_1>S_2>\dots>S_k=0$, we have $h_1>h_2>\dots>h_k=\tau+V$; a larger index means an earlier checkpoint. Independently of the copies, we draw $K\in[k]$ with $\Pr[K\ge i]=2^{1-i}$ for all $i\in[k]$; for instance, $K$ is the number of fair coin flips up to and including the first head, truncated at~$k$. Note that $\E[K]=\sum_{i=1}^k\Pr[K\ge i]=2-2^{1-k}$ and $\Pr[K=k]=2^{1-k}$, and let
\[
  \beta=\E[K]-\Pr[K=k]=2-2^{2-k}.
\]
The \emph{amplified} terminal distribution $\Amp_M(\cD)$ is the distribution of the terminal instance $(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)$ with all weights divided by~$\beta$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:amplification}
Let $M\ge1$ be an integer, $k=2M+1$, and $\eta=1/(2^k-2)$. If~$\cD$ is $(c,M)$-hard, then $\Amp_M(\cD)$ is $((1+\eta)c,(k+1)M)$-hard.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
All jobs of $\bar I\cup\{J\}$ are released by time $\tau<h_K$, and the support is finite, so $\Amp_M(\cD)$ is a terminal distribution. We show that the terminal instance $(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)$, before the weights are divided by~$\beta$, satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:amp-goal}
  \E\bigl[U^*(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)\bigr]\le\beta
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \E\bigl[\Phi_{(k+1)M}(A;\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)\bigr]\ge c\,\E[K]
\end{equation}
for every deterministic online algorithm~$A$, where the expectations are over the copies and~$K$. This implies the lemma by \cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:scaling} with $\lambda=\beta$, because $\Phi_{\beta(k+1)M}\ge\Phi_{(k+1)M}$ (as $\beta\ge1$), $(1+\eta)c\le2M\le(k+1)M$, and
\[
  \frac{\E[K]}{\beta}=\frac{2-2^{1-k}}{2-2^{2-k}}=\frac{2^k-1}{2^k-2}=1+\eta.
\]

\emph{Offline.} Follow the schedule of \cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:offline} until time~$\tau$. If $K=i<k$, use the interval $[\tau,h_i)$ of length $V+S_i$ to complete~$J$, of size~$V$, and the remaining jobs of copies $i+1,\dots,k$, of total size at most~$S_i$ by \cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:geometry}. Then only jobs of copies $1,\dots,i$ are unfinished at time~$h_i$, and their total weight is at most $B_1+\dots+B_i$. If $K=k$, use the interval $[\tau,\tau+V)$ to complete all remaining jobs of~$\bar I$, whose total size is at most $p(\bar I)\le V$. Then only~$J$ is unfinished at time~$h_k$, and its weight is $2M=k-1$. Write $U^*=U^*(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)$. Since~$K$ is independent of the copies and $\E[B_j]\le1$ for all~$j$, we obtain $\E[U^*\mid K=i]\le i$ for $i<k$ and $\E[U^*\mid K=k]\le k-1$. Hence $\E[U^*]\le\E[K]-\Pr[K=k]=\beta$; see the left part of \cref{fig:amp-accounting}.

\emph{Online.} Fix a deterministic online algorithm~$A$ and the copies $(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_k,T_k)$. We first take the expectation over~$K$ only, which we denote by~$\E_K$; as~$K$ is independent of the copies, $\E_K$ is the conditional expectation given the copies. The variable~$K$ affects the terminal time but not the instance, so the schedule $A(\bar I\cup\{J\})$ is fixed. Let~$E_j$ and~$X_j$ be as in \cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:online} for $\tilde I=\bar I\cup\{J\}$. Let~$\ell$ be the smallest $i\in[k]$ such that~$J$ has remaining size at least~$1$ at time~$h_i$, and let $\ell=k+1$ if there is no such~$i$. Since remaining sizes do not increase over time and $h_1>h_2>\dots>h_k$, job~$J$ has remaining size at least~$1$ at time~$h_i$ if and only if $i\ge\ell$.

We claim that for all $j<\ell$ and all $i\ge j$, every job of~$E_j$ has remaining size at least~$1$ at time~$h_i$. As $h_i\le h_j$, it suffices to consider $i=j$. Because $j<\ell$, job~$J$ has remaining size less than~$1$ at time~$h_j$, so it received more than $V-1$ processing during $[\tau,h_j)$. This leaves less than $(h_j-\tau)-(V-1)=S_j+1$ processing for all other jobs during this interval. By \cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:online}, every job of~$E_j$ has remaining size at least $S_j+2$ at time~$\tau$, and hence more than~$1$ at time~$h_j$.

By the claim, the robust backlog of~$A$ at time~$h_K$ contains~$E_j$ for all $j\le K$ with $j<\ell$, and it contains~$J$ if $K\ge\ell$; see the right part of \cref{fig:amp-accounting}. These sets are pairwise disjoint and $w(E_j)\ge X_j$, so the weight of the robust backlog is at least
\[
  Z=\sum_{j\le K,\;j<\ell}X_j+2M\cdot\bone[K\ge\ell].
\]
Moreover, $Z\le(k+1)M$: if $K<\ell$, then~$Z$ is a sum of at most~$k$ terms~$X_j\le M$, and if $K\ge\ell$, then~$Z$ is a sum of at most~$k-1$ such terms and~$2M$. Hence $\Phi_{(k+1)M}(A;\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)\ge Z$. Taking the expectation over~$K$, with~$\ell$ and all~$X_j$ fixed, we obtain
\[
  \E_K[Z]=\sum_{j<\ell}\Pr[K\ge j]\,X_j+2M\cdot\Pr[K\ge\ell].
\]
The second term compensates for the copies $j\ge\ell$, whose robust backlogs the algorithm may have cleared. Indeed, as $X_j\le M$, we have for $\ell\le k$ that
\[
  \sum_{j=\ell}^k\Pr[K\ge j]\,X_j\le M\sum_{j=\ell}^k2^{1-j}<2M\cdot2^{1-\ell}=2M\cdot\Pr[K\ge\ell],
\]
and for $\ell=k+1$, both sides vanish. Hence $\E_K[Z]\ge\sum_{j=1}^k\Pr[K\ge j]\,X_j$. Finally, we take the expectation over the copies. By \cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:online} and the law of total expectation, $\E[X_j]\ge c$ for all~$j$, so
\[
  \E\bigl[\Phi_{(k+1)M}(A;\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)\bigr]\ge\E\bigl[\E_K[Z]\bigr]\ge\sum_{j=1}^k\Pr[K\ge j]\,\E[X_j]\ge c\,\E[K].
\]
This proves~\eqref{eq:amp-goal} and the lemma.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:amplification}
Let $M=1$, and let~$\cD$ be the $(1,1)$-hard single-job distribution of \cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:base}, with the bound $H=2$. Then $k=3$, and before normalization, the amplified distribution consists of the jobs $(r_j,p_j,w_j)=(0,100,1)$, $(50,20,1)$, $(60,4,1)$, and $J=(62,124,2)$, with the checkpoints $(h_1,h_2,h_3)=(210,190,186)$, which are the terminal time with probabilities $1/2$, $1/4$, and~$1/4$. As~$\cD$ consists of a single terminal instance, the choice of the checkpoint is the only randomness, and both inequalities in~\eqref{eq:amp-goal} are tight.

\emph{Offline.} The minimum unfinished weights at $h_1$, $h_2$, and~$h_3$ are $1$, $2$, and~$2$, so their expectation is $3/2=\beta$. Indeed, the total size~$248$ exceeds~$h_1$, and leaving only a job of weight~$1$ unfinished at time~$h_2$ or~$h_3$ would require completing~$J$ and two of the other jobs; even the cheapest choice, $J$ and the jobs of sizes $20$ and~$4$, requires $148$ units of processing after time~$50$, but only $140$ are available until~$h_2$.

\emph{Online.} Consider the algorithm that processes each copy during its own time window, then~$J$, and then the remaining old jobs in the order of increasing remaining size. At $h_3=186$, it has just completed~$J$, and the three old jobs have remaining sizes $50$, $10$, and~$2$. At~$h_2$, the jobs with remaining sizes $50$ and~$8$ remain, and at~$h_1$, only the first one remains. Its robust backlogs at $h_1$, $h_2$, and~$h_3$ thus have weights $1$, $2$, and~$3$, and its expected robust backlog is $7/4=c\,\E[K]$. After normalization, the gap is $(7/4)/(3/2)=7/6$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Averaging}\label{sec:averaging}

Averaging reduces the cap to the gap. For a terminal distribution~$\cD$ with a bound~$H$ and a positive integer~$m$, the \emph{averaged} terminal distribution $\Avg_m(\cD)$ is the distribution of the terminal instance $(\bar I,\tau)$, where~$\bar I$ is the concatenation of~$m$ copies of~$\cD$ and~$\tau$ is its end, with all weights divided by~$m$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:averaging}
Let~$\cD$ be a $(c,M)$-hard terminal distribution, let $0<\eps<c$, and let $m=\lceil(M/\eps)^2\rceil$. Then $\Avg_m(\cD)$ is $(c-\eps,c)$-hard.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:geometry}, all jobs of~$\bar I$ are released before $u_m=\tau$, so $\Avg_m(\cD)$ is a terminal distribution. By \cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:scaling} with $\lambda=m$, it suffices to show that $\E[U^*(\bar I,\tau)]\le m$ and $\E[\Phi_{cm}(A;\bar I,\tau)]\ge(c-\eps)\,m$ for every deterministic online algorithm~$A$, where the weights are not divided by~$m$ and the expectations are over the copies $(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_m,T_m)$. The first bound follows from \cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:offline}, since $U^*(\bar I,\tau)\le B_1+\dots+B_m$.

Fix~$A$, and let~$E_i$ and~$X_i$ be as in \cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:online} for $\tilde I=\bar I$. Every job of~$E_i$ has remaining size at least $S_i+2\ge2$ at time~$\tau$, so the robust backlog of~$A$ at time~$\tau$ contains the pairwise disjoint sets $E_1,\dots,E_m$, and
\[
  \Phi_{cm}(A;\bar I,\tau)\ge\min\{cm,Y\},\qquad\text{where}\qquad Y=\sum_{i=1}^mX_i.
\]
For $i\in\{0,\dots,m\}$, let~$\cF_i$ be the $\sigma$-algebra generated by $(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_i,T_i)$, so that~$\cF_0$ is trivial. Let $\mu_i=\E[X_i\mid\cF_{i-1}]$, and let $\Delta=\sum_{i=1}^m(X_i-\mu_i)$. By \cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:online}, $\mu_i\ge c$, so $Y\ge cm+\Delta$. The variables~$X_i$ need not be independent, since the algorithm adapts to the past, but the differences $X_i-\mu_i$ form a martingale difference sequence: $X_i-\mu_i$ is $\cF_i$-measurable, $\E[X_i-\mu_i\mid\cF_{i-1}]=0$, and $|X_i-\mu_i|\le M$ because $X_i$ and~$\mu_i$ lie in $[0,M]$. For $i<j$, conditioning on~$\cF_{j-1}$ shows $\E[(X_i-\mu_i)(X_j-\mu_j)]=0$, so $\E[\Delta^2]=\sum_{i=1}^m\E[(X_i-\mu_i)^2]\le mM^2$. Using $(cm-Y)^+\le(-\Delta)^+\le|\Delta|$ and the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality, we conclude
\[
  \E\bigl[\min\{cm,Y\}\bigr]=cm-\E\bigl[(cm-Y)^+\bigr]\ge cm-\E\bigl[|\Delta|\bigr]\ge cm-\sqrt{\E[\Delta^2]}\ge cm-M\sqrt m\ge(c-\eps)\,m,
\]
where the last inequality uses $m\ge(M/\eps)^2$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of \texorpdfstring{\cref{thm:hard}}{Theorem 3.4}}\label{sec:iteration}

Let $R\ge1$. We use the cap $M=\lceil4R\rceil$, the number $k=2M+1$ of copies in amplification and the parameter $\eta=1/(2^k-2)$ of \cref{lem:amplification}, and the number $m=\lceil(2(k+1)M/\eta)^2\rceil$ of copies in averaging. Let~$\cD_0$ be the $(1,M)$-hard terminal distribution of \cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:base}, and for $q\ge1$, let $\cD_q=\Avg_m(\Amp_M(\cD_{q-1}))$, where both operations may use any bound for their input distribution; \cref{sec:quantitative} fixes specific bounds. We call the step from~$\cD_{q-1}$ to~$\cD_q$ the $q$-th \emph{round}. Let $c_q=(1+\eta/2)^q$, and let~$N$ be the smallest integer with $c_N\ge2R$.

We show by induction that~$\cD_q$ is $(c_q,M)$-hard for $q=0,\dots,N$. The case $q=0$ holds. Let $1\le q\le N$, and assume that~$\cD_{q-1}$ is $(c_{q-1},M)$-hard. By \cref{lem:amplification}, $\Amp_M(\cD_{q-1})$ is $((1+\eta)c_{q-1},(k+1)M)$-hard. We apply \cref{lem:averaging} with $\eps=\eta/2<(1+\eta)c_{q-1}$, for which the number of copies is $\lceil((k+1)M/\eps)^2\rceil=m$. Hence~$\cD_q$ is $((1+\eta)c_{q-1}-\eta/2,\,(1+\eta)c_{q-1})$-hard. Since $c_{q-1}\ge1$, we have $(1+\eta)c_{q-1}-\eta/2\ge(1+\eta/2)c_{q-1}=c_q$. Since $q-1<N$, we have $c_{q-1}<2R$, and as $\eta<1$, the cap satisfies $(1+\eta)c_{q-1}<4R\le M$. By \cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:monotone}, $\cD_q$ is $(c_q,M)$-hard.

In particular, $\cD_N$ is $(c_N,M)$-hard and hence $(2R,M)$-hard by \cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:monotone}. This proves \cref{thm:hard}.

By induction on~$q$, all instances in the support of~$\cD_q$ have the same number of jobs, and flooding (\cref{lem:flooding}) adds the same number of jobs to each instance. Hence our lower bounds hold even for algorithms that know the number of jobs in advance.

\section{Dependence on \texorpdfstring{$W$, $P$, and~$n$}{W, P, and n}}\label{sec:quantitative}

For an instance~$I$, let $W(I)=\max_{j\in I}w_j/\min_{j\in I}w_j$ and $P(I)=\max_{j\in I}p_j/\min_{j\in I}p_j$ be its weight and size ratios, and let~$|I|$ be its number of jobs. In this section, all logarithms are binary. We analyze the construction of \cref{sec:iteration} and prove the following refinement of \cref{thm:main}.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:quant-refined}
There is an absolute constant $C\ge1$ such that for all $R\ge1$ and $\gamma\ge0$, there is a probability distribution~$\hat\cD$ over finitely many instances such that every instance~$\hat I$ in its support satisfies
\[
  W(\hat I)\le2^{2^{CR}},\qquad P(\hat I)\le2^{2^{2^{CR}}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad|\hat I|\le2^{2^{2^{CR}}},
\]
and every deterministic online algorithm~$A$ satisfies $\E_{\hat I\sim\hat\cD}[F_A(\hat I)]\ge R\cdot\E_{\hat I\sim\hat\cD}[\OPT(\hat I)]+\gamma$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $M$, $k$, $\eta$, $m$, $N$, and $\cD_0,\dots,\cD_N$ be as in \cref{sec:iteration}, and let $\beta=2-2^{2-k}$ be the normalization factor of amplification. We obtain~$\hat\cD$ by flooding~$\cD_N$. After a reduction to $\gamma=1$, we bound the weight and size ratios and the number of jobs in terms of $M$, $k$, $m$, and~$N$, and finally we bound these parameters in terms of~$R$.

We may assume that $\gamma=1$. Indeed, let~$\hat\cD$ satisfy the theorem for $\gamma=1$, let $\lambda\ge1$, and for an instance~$I$, let~$\lambda I$ be the instance with all weights multiplied by~$\lambda$, which has the same weight and size ratios and the same number of jobs as~$I$. For a deterministic online algorithm~$A$, the algorithm~$A'$ with $A'(I)=A(\lambda I)$ is a deterministic online algorithm, as in the proof of \cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:scaling}. As $F_A(\lambda I)=\lambda F_{A'}(I)$ and $\OPT(\lambda I)=\lambda\OPT(I)$, we obtain $\E_{\hat I\sim\hat\cD}[F_A(\lambda\hat I)]\ge R\cdot\E_{\hat I\sim\hat\cD}[\OPT(\lambda\hat I)]+\lambda$. Hence the distribution of~$\lambda\hat I$ satisfies the theorem for every $\gamma\le\lambda$.

We apply \cref{lem:flooding} with $\gamma=1$ to the $(2R,M)$-hard terminal distribution~$\cD_N$, which yields~$\hat\cD$; we fix the length of the stream below. It remains to bound the weight and size ratios and the number of jobs. Recall that the stream jobs have weight~$1/2$ and size $1/(2M)$.

\emph{Weights.} We show by induction that for $q\ge0$, all weights in~$\cD_q$ lie in $[(\beta m)^{-q},1]$. The only weight in~$\cD_0$ is~$1$. Amplification keeps the weights of the copies, adds the weight~$2M$, and divides all weights by~$\beta$; averaging then divides all weights by~$m$. Hence the weights in~$\cD_q$ lie in $[w_{\min}/(\beta m),\,\max\{w_{\max},2M\}/(\beta m)]$, where $w_{\min}$ and~$w_{\max}$ are the smallest and largest weights in~$\cD_{q-1}$. The claim follows because $w_{\max}\le1$ and $2M\le m\le\beta m$. We have $N\ge1$ because $c_0=1<2R$, so $(\beta m)^{-N}\le1/2$, and the stream weight~$1/2$ also lies in $[(\beta m)^{-N},1]$. Thus $W(\hat I)\le(\beta m)^N\le(2m)^N$.

\emph{Sizes.} We fix the bounds used by the construction. Let $H_0=2$, and for $q\ge1$, let $\hat H_q=3((2H_{q-1}+1)^k-1)$ and $H_q=(2\hat H_q+1)^m-1$. In round~$q$, amplification uses the bound~$H_{q-1}$ for~$\cD_{q-1}$, and averaging uses the bound~$\hat H_q$ for $\Amp_M(\cD_{q-1})$. These bounds are valid: $H_0$ bounds the terminal time and the size in~$\cD_0$. If~$H_{q-1}$ bounds the terminal times and total sizes in~$\cD_{q-1}$, then in $\Amp_M(\cD_{q-1})$, the concatenation of~$k$ copies has total size at most $V=(2H_{q-1}+1)^k-1$ by \cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:geometry}, so the total size including~$J$ is at most~$2V$, and the terminal time is at most $h_1=\tau+V+S_1\le3V=\hat H_q$. Applying \cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:geometry} to the $m$ copies of averaging shows that~$H_q$ bounds the terminal times and total sizes in~$\cD_q$. Moreover, all jobs in~$\cD_q$ have size at least~$2$: this holds for~$\cD_0$, copies stretch sizes by factors $s_i\ge2$, and the job~$J$ added by amplification has size $V=\rho^k-1\ge\rho-1=2H_{q-1}\ge2$. Consequently, all sizes in~$\hat I$ lie in $[1/(2M),H_N]$, and $P(\hat I)\le2MH_N$.

To bound~$H_N$, let $G_q=2H_q+1$, so that $G_0=5$ and $G_q\ge5$ for all~$q$. Since $2\hat H_q+1\le6\,G_{q-1}^k$ and $2\cdot6^m\le25^m\le G_{q-1}^{2m}$, we have
\[
  G_q\le2\,(2\hat H_q+1)^m\le2\cdot6^m\,G_{q-1}^{km}\le G_{q-1}^{(k+2)m},
\]
and therefore $\log H_N<\log G_N\le((k+2)m)^N\log5$.

\emph{Number of jobs.} Every instance in the support of~$\cD_N$ consists of jobs of size at least~$2$ and has total size at most~$H_N$, so it has at most $H_N/2$ jobs, and as its weights are at most~$1$, its total weight is at most~$H_N$. Hence the proof of \cref{lem:flooding} for $\gamma=1$ applies with $H=Q=H_N$ and $L=4H_N^2+2$, that is, with $2M(4H_N^2+2)$ stream jobs. As $M\ge4$ and $H_N\ge2$, we obtain $|\hat I|\le H_N/2+2M(4H_N^2+2)\le(2MH_N)^2$.

\emph{Growth in~$R$.} We have $M\le5R$ and $k\le11R$. Since $1/\eta<2^k$, we get $\log m\le2k+2\log(2(k+1)M)+1=O(R)$, and since $\ln(1+x)\ge x/2$ for $x\in[0,1]$, the number of rounds satisfies
\[
  N\le1+\frac{\ln(2R)}{\ln(1+\eta/2)}\le1+\frac{4\ln(2R)}{\eta}\le1+2^{k+2}\ln(2R)=2^{O(R)}.
\]
Hence $\log W(\hat I)\le N\log(2m)=2^{O(R)}$. Moreover, using $\log(a+b)\le1+\log a+\log b$ for $a,b\ge1$,
\begin{align*}
  \log\log(2MH_N)
  &\le\log\bigl(\log(2M)+((k+2)m)^N\log5\bigr)\\
  &\le1+\log\log(2M)+N\log((k+2)m)+\log\log5=2^{O(R)}.
\end{align*}
As $P(\hat I)\le2MH_N$ and $|\hat I|\le(2MH_N)^2$, we have $\log\log P(\hat I)\le\log\log(2MH_N)$ and $\log\log|\hat I|\le1+\log\log(2MH_N)$. All constants in the $O$-notation are absolute, so there is an absolute constant $C\ge1$ with $\log W(\hat I)\le2^{CR}$, $\log\log P(\hat I)\le2^{CR}$, and $\log\log|\hat I|\le2^{CR}$ for all $R\ge1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:quant}]
Let $W,P,n\ge16$, let~$C$ be the constant of \cref{thm:quant-refined}, let~$\cA$ be a randomized online algorithm, and let $R=\min\{\log\log W,\log\log\log P,\log\log\log n\}/C$, which is positive because $W,P,n\ge16$. If $R<1$, there is nothing to show, since no algorithm has competitive ratio less than~$1$. Otherwise, let $\Gamma\ge0$. By \cref{thm:quant-refined} with $\gamma=\Gamma+1$ and \cref{lem:yao}, there is an instance~$\hat I$ with $W(\hat I)\le2^{2^{CR}}\le W$, $P(\hat I)\le2^{2^{2^{CR}}}\le P$, and $|\hat I|\le2^{2^{2^{CR}}}\le n$ such that $\E_{A\sim\cA}[F_A(\hat I)]\ge R\cdot\OPT(\hat I)+\Gamma+1$. Hence, on instances with at most~$n$ jobs and weight and size ratios at most~$W$ and~$P$, the algorithm~$\cA$ is not $R$-competitive, and its competitive ratio is at least $R=\Omega(\min\{\log\log W,\log\log\log P,\log\log\log n\})$.
\end{proof}

\section{Concluding remarks}\label{sec:conclusion}

We have shown that randomization does not allow a constant competitive ratio for online weighted flow time on a single machine. Our lower bound of $\Omega(\min\{\log\log W,\log\log\log P\})$ is exponentially weaker than the deterministic lower bound of Bansal and Chan~\cite{BansalC09}, and the best known upper bound is $O(\log\min\{P,D,W\})$, achieved by a deterministic algorithm~\cite{AzarT18}. The weak dependence on~$W$ and~$P$ stems from the amplification step: it improves the gap only by the factor $1+\eta$, where $\eta=1/(2^{2M+1}-2)$ is exponentially small in the cap~$M$, so $2^{\Theta(R)}$ rounds are needed to reach the gap~$2R$. An amplification step whose improvement~$\eta$ is only polynomially small in the cap would lead to lower bounds that are polynomial in $\log W$ and $\log\log P$. More generally, it remains open whether randomized algorithms can beat the deterministic lower bound, for example by achieving a competitive ratio of $O(\log\log W)$.

In terms of the number~$n$ of jobs, our lower bound $\Omega(\log\log\log n)$ is weaker still, because flooding dominates the number of jobs. Each round multiplies the number of jobs by at most $m(k+1)$, so the instances in the support of~$\cD_N$ have at most $(m(k+1))^N=2^{2^{O(R)}}$ jobs. The stream, however, has to outlast these instances, so that their cost before the terminal time is negligible, and it consists of more than~$P$ jobs. A conversion of gaps at the terminal time into gaps in flow time that adds only polynomially many jobs would therefore give $\Omega(\log\log n)$. Whether a single machine admits an $O(\log n)$-competitive algorithm remains open; on $m\ge2$ machines, the lower bound of Chekuri, Khanna, and Zhu~\cite{ChekuriKZ01} rules out a polylogarithmic dependence on~$n$.
