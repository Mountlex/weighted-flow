\title{Weighted Flow Time Does Not Admit\\ $O(1)$-Competitive Randomized Algorithms}

\author{%

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We study online preemptive scheduling of jobs with release times, sizes, and weights on a single machine, with the objective of minimizing total weighted flow time. Bansal and Chan (SODA~2009) showed that no deterministic online algorithm is $O(1)$-competitive for this problem. We show that no randomized online algorithm is $O(1)$-competitive against an oblivious adversary. Quantitatively, every randomized algorithm has competitive ratio $\Omega(\min\{\log\log W,\log\log\log\min\{P,D\}\})$, where $W$, $P$, and~$D$ are the ratios between the largest and the smallest job weight, size, and density (weight divided by size), respectively. We also obtain a high-probability lower bound with the same asymptotic dependence on the instance parameters.

Following Yao's principle, we construct, for each target ratio, a finite distribution over instances that defeats every deterministic algorithm. The construction compares the algorithm's unfinished weight at a random time not revealed to it with the minimum unfinished weight attainable at that time. Starting from a single job, it alternates two operations on independent copies at decreasing time scales. Amplification appends a heavy job and draws the comparison time from a truncated geometric distribution over checkpoints, forcing the algorithm to leave either the heavy job or its earlier backlog unfinished. Its gain is exponentially small in a cap on the backlog weight that we count, and it raises this cap. Averaging divides the weights of many copies by their number, and a second-moment bound shows that this restores the cap at a small loss.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

We consider the online problem of minimizing total weighted flow time on a single machine. Each job~$j$ has a release time~$r_j$, a size~$p_j$, and a weight~$w_j$, which become known at time~$r_j$. The machine processes at most one job at a time, and a job may be preempted and resumed later. If job~$j$ completes at time~$C_j$, its flow time $C_j-r_j$ is the time that it spends in the system, and the objective is to minimize $\sum_j w_j(C_j-r_j)$. An online algorithm decides at every time which job to process, without knowledge of future jobs. It is \emph{$\rho$-competitive} if, on every instance, its (expected) cost is at most $\rho$ times the optimal cost, up to an additive constant.

Without weights, always processing the job with the shortest remaining processing time is optimal, even online~\cite{Schrage68}. With weights, the problem is considerably harder. Already the offline problem is strongly NP-hard~\cite{LabetoulleLLR84}. A polynomial-time approximation scheme~\cite{ArmbrusterRW23} followed a long line of work~\cite{ChekuriK02,BansalP10,BatraGK18,FeigeKL19,RohwedderW21}.

In the online setting, the first algorithm with a nontrivial competitive ratio is due to Chekuri, Khanna, and Zhu~\cite{ChekuriKZ01}. It is $O(\log^2P)$-competitive, where $P$ is the ratio between the largest and the smallest job size, and it needs to know~$P$ in advance. Bansal and Dhamdhere~\cite{BansalD07} gave an $O(\log W)$-competitive algorithm, where $W$ is the ratio between the largest and the smallest weight. Azar and Touitou~\cite{AzarT18} gave an $O(\log P)$-competitive and an $O(\log D)$-competitive algorithm, where $D$ is the ratio between the largest and the smallest density $w_j/p_j$. They combined these results with the algorithm of Bansal and Dhamdhere into an $O(\log\min\{P,D,W\})$-competitive algorithm. A constant competitive ratio is possible with a slightly faster machine. On a machine that is faster by a factor of $1+\eps$, the Highest Density First rule is $O(1/\eps)$-competitive~\cite{BecchettiLMP06}.

It was widely believed that weighted flow time admits an $O(1)$-competitive online algorithm, until Bansal and Chan~\cite{BansalC09} showed that every deterministic online algorithm has competitive ratio
\begin{equation*}
  \Omega\biggl(\min\biggl\{\sqrt{\frac{\log W}{\log\log W}},\,\sqrt{\frac{\log\log P}{\log\log\log P}}\biggr\}\biggr)\,.
\end{equation*}
Their proof starts from the trivial lower bound of one and improves it step by step. Their adversary is adaptive and chooses the next jobs depending on the decisions that the algorithm has made so far. Against a randomized algorithm, however, an oblivious adversary has to fix the instance in advance, without knowing the algorithm's random choices. The adaptive construction therefore does not apply.

Randomization can be powerful when job sizes remain unknown until completion. In this model, every deterministic algorithm for unweighted flow time on a single machine has competitive ratio $\Omega(n^{1/3})$, where $n$ is the number of jobs~\cite{MotwaniPT94}. A randomized version of the multilevel feedback algorithm~\cite{KalyanasundaramP03} is $O(\log n)$-competitive~\cite{BecchettiL04}, which is best possible~\cite{MotwaniPT94}.

For weighted flow time, however, Chekuri, Khanna, and Zhu~\cite{ChekuriKZ01} showed that no randomized online algorithm is $O(1)$-competitive on $m\ge2$ identical machines, proving a lower bound of $\Omega(\min\{\sqrt P,\sqrt W,(n/m)^{1/4}\})$. On a single machine, the only lower bound for randomized algorithms that we are aware of is the constant~$4/3$, also due to Chekuri, Khanna, and Zhu. Thus it has remained open whether a randomized online algorithm can be $O(1)$-competitive for weighted flow time on a single machine.

\paragraph{Our results.}
We show that this is not the case.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
No randomized online algorithm for minimizing the total weighted flow time on a single machine is $O(1)$-competitive against an oblivious adversary. More precisely, for all $\rho\ge1$ and $\gamma\ge0$, there is a probability distribution~$\cD$ over finitely many instances such that every deterministic online algorithm~$A$ satisfies
\begin{equation*}
  \E_{I\sim\cD}\bigl[F_A(I)\bigr]\ge \rho\cdot\E_{I\sim\cD}\bigl[\OPT(I)\bigr]+\gamma\,.
\end{equation*}
\end{theorem}

Here $F_A(I)$ denotes the weighted flow time of the schedule that $A$ produces on the instance~$I$, and $\OPT(I)$ is the optimal weighted flow time of~$I$. By Yao's principle, the second statement implies the first, and the term~$\gamma$ rules out additive constants. The lower bound holds even when the algorithm learns each job's size and weight at its release, may process several jobs simultaneously at fractional rates, and knows the distribution~$\cD$ in advance. In particular, it may know upper bounds on~$W$, $P$, and~$D$, as well as the number of jobs, which is the same for all instances in the support of~$\cD$.

Thus, even with randomization, the competitive ratio has to grow with the parameters of the instance. Our construction gives an explicit, if weak, dependence on~$W$, $P$, $D$, and the number~$n$ of jobs.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:quant}
For all $W,P,D,n\ge16$, every randomized online algorithm for weighted flow time has competitive ratio
\begin{equation*}
  \Omega\bigl(\min\{\log\log W,\,\log\log\log\min\{P,D,n\}\}\bigr)
\end{equation*}
against an oblivious adversary on instances with at most~$n$ jobs and weight, size, and density ratios at most~$W$, $P$, and~$D$, respectively.
\end{theorem}

In particular, for $W=P=D=n$, the competitive ratio on instances with at most~$n$ jobs is $\Omega(\log\log\log n)$. The lower bound also holds with high probability. For every $\rho\ge1$, there is an instance on which the algorithm incurs at least $\rho$ times the optimal cost with probability at least $1-2^{-\rho}$, with the same asymptotic bounds on the instance parameters~(\cref{cor:probability}).

The dependence on~$W$ and~$P$ is exponentially weaker than in the deterministic lower bound of Bansal and Chan: to reach the lower bound~$\rho$, our construction proceeds in steps that each improve the bound only by a factor of $1+2^{-\Theta(\rho)}$. We discuss this dependence in~\cref{sec:conclusion}.

\subsection{Technical overview}\label{sec:overview}

By Yao's principle, it suffices to construct, for every~$\rho$, a distribution for which every deterministic algorithm has expected cost at least $\rho$ times the expected optimal cost. The instance cannot react to the algorithm, so the adversary cannot choose later jobs based on which earlier jobs remain unfinished. Instead, our distributions hide the time at which the algorithm's unfinished weight is compared with the minimum possible unfinished weight.

\paragraph{Unfinished weight at a hidden time.}
Weighted flow time is the integral of the \emph{unfinished weight}, the total weight of released but uncompleted jobs. Controlling this weight at every time therefore suffices for competitiveness. Conversely, Becchetti et al.~\cite{BecchettiLMP06} showed that every $c$-competitive deterministic online algorithm is also $c$-competitive for minimizing unfinished weight at an unknown deadline. We use this converse direction: we create a large expected gap in unfinished weight at a hidden time, and a stream of small jobs then makes this gap persist long enough to dominate the weighted flow time.

\paragraph{Hard terminal distributions.}
Our building blocks are \emph{terminal distributions} over pairs $(I,T)$, where~$T$ is later than all release times in~$I$. The algorithm sees the jobs of~$I$ as they arrive, but is not given~$T$. Offline, $U^*(I,T)$ is the minimum unfinished weight at~$T$. Online, we count only \emph{robust} jobs, whose remaining size at~$T$ is at least~$1$. These jobs form the \emph{robust backlog} and cannot finish before~$T+1$. We cap their total weight at~$M$. A terminal distribution is \emph{$(c,M)$-hard} if $\E[U^*(I,T)]\le1$, whereas every deterministic algorithm has expected capped robust backlog at least~$c$ (\cref{def:hard}). We call~$c$ the \emph{gap} and~$M$ the \emph{cap}; the cap is needed for amplification.

\paragraph{Flooding.}
A $(2\rho,M)$-hard terminal distribution yields the lower bound~$\rho$ by \emph{flooding} (\cref{lem:flooding}). Starting at~$T$, a long stream of jobs of weight~$1/2$ and density~$M$ keeps the machine busy. Until the algorithm has spent one unit of processing on old jobs, its robust backlog remains unfinished; afterwards, the stream jobs that it has delayed have total weight at least~$M$. Hence its unfinished weight never drops below its capped robust backlog at~$T$. Offline can instead leave weight $U^*(I,T)$ unfinished and process each stream job immediately, keeping unfinished weight at $U^*(I,T)+1/2$. A sufficiently long stream makes this gap dominate the costs before and after it.

It thus suffices to construct $(c,M)$-hard terminal distributions with arbitrarily large gap~$c$. We start from the trivial $(1,M)$-hard distribution that consists of a single job of size~$2$ and weight~$1$, released at time~$0$, with terminal time~$1$, and we alternate two operations, amplification and averaging. Both build on the following way of combining copies of a hard distribution.

\paragraph{Copies at decreasing time scales.}
We concatenate independent samples of a hard distribution, starting copy~$i$ at the terminal time of copy~$i-1$ and stretching its times and sizes by~$s_i$. Measured at this scale, its robust backlog consists of jobs with remaining size at least~$s_i$. All later copies together have duration and total size less than~$s_i/2$ (\cref{fig:amplification}), so this backlog survives them. Given the earlier copies, copy~$i$ remains a fresh sample, and later copies arrive only from its terminal time on. Thus its capped robust backlog~$X_i$ has conditional expectation at least~$c$, even though the algorithm adapts to the past (\cref{fig:copies}). Offline can run each copy's schedule in its own window, leaving expected weight at most~$1$ per copy (\cref{lem:copies}).

\paragraph{Amplification.}
Amplification takes $k=2M+1$ copies of a $(c,M)$-hard distribution and, right after them, releases a heavy job~$J$ of weight~$2M$ whose size~$V$ is at least the total size of all copies. The terminal time is one of $k$ checkpoints $h_k<h_{k-1}<\dots<h_1$ after the release of~$J$, where checkpoint~$h_i$ leaves enough time to complete~$J$ and copies $i+1,\dots,k$. We choose the checkpoint~$h_K$ at random, with $\Pr[K\ge i]=2^{1-i}$ for $i=1,\dots,k$. For the terminal time~$h_i$, an offline schedule can complete~$J$ and copies $i+1,\dots,k$, which leaves expected weight at most~$i$ unfinished. For the earliest checkpoint~$h_k$, it can instead complete all copies and leave only~$J$, of weight $k-1$, unfinished. Hence the minimum unfinished weight at the terminal time is at most $\E[K]-\Pr[K=k]$ in expectation.

At checkpoint~$h_i$, either~$J$ is still robust, or every job in copy~$i$'s robust backlog is. In the latter case, this backlog contributes at~$h_i$ and at all earlier checkpoints. In the former case, $J$ compensates for the possibly missing contribution of copy~$i$: its weight is twice the cap of a copy, and the geometric probabilities make this enough. Charging each copy separately gives expected capped robust backlog at least $c\,\E[K]$ (\cref{fig:amp-accounting}). Offline saves $\Pr[K=k]$ relative to~$\E[K]$, so normalizing the weights improves the gap by at least a factor of $1+\eta$, where $\eta=2^{-k}$~(\cref{lem:amplification}).

\paragraph{Averaging.}
Amplification improves the gap but increases the cap, making the next improvement exponentially smaller. Averaging brings the cap back down. Given a $(c,M)$-hard distribution, it concatenates~$m$ copies and divides all weights by~$m$. Their capped robust backlogs $X_i\in[0,M]$ need not be independent, but have conditional expectations at least~$c$. A second-moment bound shows that their average, capped at~$c$, has expectation at least $c-M/\sqrt m$ (\cref{lem:averaging}). Thus we can reduce the cap to~$c$ while losing arbitrarily little from the gap.

\paragraph{Putting things together.}
We fix the cap $M=\lceil4\rho\rceil$ and alternate amplification and averaging. Amplification increases the gap by at least~$\eta$, and averaging loses at most~$\eta/2$ while returning the cap to at most~$M$. Each round therefore adds at least~$\eta/2$ to the gap. After $2^{O(\rho)}$ rounds it reaches~$2\rho$~(\cref{thm:hard}). Each round combines $2^{O(\rho)}$ copies, giving the bounds on~$W$, $P$, $D$, and~$n$ in~\cref{thm:quant}.

\paragraph{Comparison with the deterministic lower bound.}
Our construction builds on the deterministic lower bound of Bansal and Chan~\cite{BansalC09}, who introduced the idea of improving a lower bound step by step. Several of our ingredients appear there in adaptive form. They measure unfinished weight using jobs with large remaining size, run subinstances at decreasing scales, release a final heavy job, and convert unfinished weight into flow time with a stream of small jobs. Their adversary observes the algorithm. Each subinstance ends when the algorithm has fallen behind, and the continuation after the heavy job depends on how the algorithm processes it. Our hidden random terminal time makes amplification oblivious. The conditional analysis of copies (\cref{lem:copies}) handles algorithms that adapt to earlier copies, while the cap and averaging make amplification repeatable although its guarantee holds only in expectation.

\subsection{Further related work}

Without weights, the Shortest Remaining Processing Time rule is $O(\log\min\{P,n/m\})$-competitive on $m$ identical machines, which is best possible for $m\ge2$, even for randomized algorithms~\cite{LeonardiR07}. Related models include weighted flow time with speed augmentation~\cite{BecchettiLMP06,AnandGK12} and flow time with uncertain job sizes~\cite{AzarLT21,AzarLT22}.

\Cref{sec:prelim} introduces the model and Yao's principle. \Cref{sec:hard} defines hard terminal distributions and shows that they imply \cref{thm:main}. \Cref{sec:construction} constructs hard terminal distributions, \cref{sec:quantitative} bounds the instance parameters and proves the high-probability corollary, and~\cref{sec:conclusion} discusses open problems.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec:prelim}

\paragraph{Instances and schedules.}
An \emph{instance}~$I$ is a finite set of jobs. Each job $j\in I$ has a release time $r_j\ge0$, a size $p_j>0$, and a weight $w_j>0$. We identify a job with the triple $(r_j,p_j,w_j)$, so an algorithm knows nothing about a job but these parameters. For a set~$E$ of jobs, we write $w(E)=\sum_{j\in E}w_j$ and $p(E)=\sum_{j\in E}p_j$. For a positive integer~$n$, we write $[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}$.

For~$\lambda>0$, let $I/\lambda$ denote the instance obtained by replacing each job $(r_j,p_j,w_j)$ of~$I$ by $(r_j,p_j,w_j/\lambda)$. We define~$\lambda I$ analogously, with weights~$\lambda w_j$.

A \emph{schedule} of~$I$ assigns to each job $j\in I$ a measurable rate function $x_j\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,1]$ such that $x_j(t)=0$ for $t<r_j$, $\sum_{j\in I}x_j(t)\le1$ for all~$t$, and $\int_0^\infty x_j(t)\dd t\le p_j$. The \emph{remaining size} of~$j$ at time~$t$ is $p_j-\int_0^t x_j(t')\dd t'$, a continuous and nonincreasing function of~$t$. Job~$j$ \emph{completes} at the first time~$C_j$ at which its remaining size is zero ($C_j=\infty$ if there is no such time), and it is \emph{unfinished} at time~$t$ if $r_j\le t<C_j$. The weighted flow time of the schedule is
\begin{equation*}
  \sum_{j\in I}w_j(C_j-r_j)=\int_0^\infty U(t)\dd t\,,
\end{equation*}
where the \emph{unfinished weight} $U(t)$ is the total weight of the jobs that are unfinished at time~$t$. We denote by $\OPT(I)$ the infimum of the weighted flow time over all schedules of~$I$.

Schedules with rates in $\{0,1\}$ are the usual preemptive schedules. Allowing fractional rates does not change the optimum. Given a fractional schedule, the preemptive Earliest Deadline First rule, with that schedule's completion times as deadlines, completes every job no later than in the given schedule. For online algorithms, fractional rates only strengthen our lower bounds.

\paragraph{Online algorithms.}
A \emph{deterministic online algorithm}~$A$ maps every instance~$I$ to a schedule $A(I)$ of~$I$ subject to the following non-anticipation condition: for every $t\ge0$ and all instances $I$ and $I'$ that contain the same jobs with release times before~$t$, the schedules $A(I)$ and $A(I')$ assign the same rates to these jobs during $[0,t)$. In other words, the decisions of~$A$ before time~$t$ depend only on the jobs released before~$t$, including their sizes and weights. In particular, these jobs have the same remaining sizes at time~$t$ in $A(I)$ and in $A(I')$. We write $F_A(I)$ for the weighted flow time of $A(I)$. A deterministic online algorithm may be designed for a specific input distribution. For example, it may use the number of jobs if this number is the same for all instances in the support. It is nevertheless defined on all instances, because our proofs also run it on auxiliary instances, for example in~\cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:scaling} and~\cref{lem:copies}.

A \emph{randomized online algorithm}~$\cA$ is a probability distribution over deterministic online algorithms such that $F_A(I)$ is a random variable for every instance~$I$ when $A\sim\cA$. It is \emph{$\rho$-competitive} (against an oblivious adversary) if there is a constant~$\Gamma$ such that $\E_{A\sim\cA}[F_A(I)]\le \rho\cdot\OPT(I)+\Gamma$ for every instance~$I$. We use the easy direction of Yao's principle~\cite{Yao77} (see also~\cite{BorodinE98}).

\begin{lemma}[Yao's principle]\label{lem:yao}
Let $\cD$ be a probability distribution over finitely many instances, and let $\rho>0$ and $\gamma\in\RR$ be such that every deterministic online algorithm~$A$ satisfies $\E_{I\sim\cD}[F_A(I)]\ge \rho\cdot\E_{I\sim\cD}[\OPT(I)]+\gamma$. Then for every randomized online algorithm~$\cA$, there is an instance~$I$ in the support of~$\cD$ with $\E_{A\sim\cA}[F_A(I)]\ge \rho\cdot\OPT(I)+\gamma$.
\end{lemma}



\section{Hard terminal distributions}\label{sec:hard}

We first define hardness at a hidden terminal time and show how it yields a lower bound for weighted flow time. This reduces~\cref{thm:main} to the construction of hard terminal distributions in~\cref{sec:construction}.

A \emph{terminal instance} is a pair $(I,T)$ of an instance~$I$ and a \emph{terminal time} $T>0$ such that all jobs of~$I$ are released before~$T$. A \emph{terminal distribution} is a probability distribution over finitely many terminal instances. An online algorithm processes only~$I$, so its schedule $A(I)$ does not depend on~$T$. The algorithm may know the distribution in advance. Since~$I$ and~$T$ can be correlated, it may also learn something about~$T$ from the jobs it sees.

For a terminal instance $(I,T)$, let $U^*(I,T)$ be the minimum total weight of the jobs that a schedule of~$I$ leaves unfinished at time~$T$. A job is \emph{robust} at time~$t$ in a schedule if its remaining size at~$t$ is at least~$1$. For a deterministic online algorithm~$A$, the \emph{robust backlog} $R_A(I,T)$ of~$A$ at time~$T$ is the set of jobs of~$I$ that are robust at time~$T$ in~$A(I)$. These jobs are unfinished at time~$T$, and none of them can be completed before time~$T+1$. For $M>0$, the \emph{capped robust backlog} is
\begin{equation*}
  \varphi_A^M(I,T):=\min\bigl\{M,\,w(R_A(I,T))\bigr\}\,.
\end{equation*}

\begin{definition}\label{def:hard}
Let $0<c\le M$. A terminal distribution~$\cD$ is \emph{$(c,M)$-hard} if
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
	\item\label{def:hard-offline} $\E_{(I,T)\sim\cD}\bigl[U^*(I,T)\bigr]\le1$ and
	\item\label{def:hard-online} $\E_{(I,T)\sim\cD}\bigl[\varphi_A^M(I,T)\bigr]\ge c$ for every deterministic online algorithm~$A$.
\end{enumerate}
We call~$c$ the \emph{gap} and~$M$ the \emph{cap}.
\end{definition}

The cap is needed for amplification (\cref{sec:amplification}). The following observation gives the starting point of our construction and two basic properties of hardness.

\begin{observation}\label{obs:basic}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item\label{obs:base} Let~$I$ consist of a single job with release time~$0$, size~$2$, and weight~$1$, and let $T=1$. The terminal distribution supported on the single terminal instance $(I,T)$ is $(1,M)$-hard for every $M\ge1$.
\item\label{obs:monotone} If~$\cD$ is $(c,M)$-hard, then~$\cD$ is $(c',M')$-hard for all $0<c'\le c$ and $M'\ge M$.
\item\label{obs:scaling} Let $\lambda>0$ and $0<c\le M$, and let~$\cD$ be a terminal distribution such that $\E_{(I,T)\sim\cD}[U^*(I,T)]\le\lambda$ and $\E_{(I,T)\sim\cD}[\varphi_A^{\lambda M}(I,T)]\ge\lambda c$ for every deterministic online algorithm~$A$. Then the distribution of $(I/\lambda,T)$ for $(I,T)\sim\cD$ is $(c,M)$-hard.
\end{enumerate}
\end{observation}

\begin{proof}
\ref{obs:base}~No schedule completes the job by time~$1$, and in every schedule its remaining size at time~$1$ is at least~$1$. Hence $U^*(I,T)=1$ and $\varphi_A^M(I,T)=\min\{M,1\}=1$.

\ref{obs:monotone}~\Cref{def:hard}\ref{def:hard-offline} involves neither the gap nor the cap, so it carries over from~$(c,M)$ to~$(c',M')$. For \cref{def:hard}\ref{def:hard-online}, since $M'\ge M$, every deterministic online algorithm~$A$ and every terminal instance $(I,T)$ satisfy $\varphi_A^{M'}(I,T)\ge\varphi_A^M(I,T)$. Taking expectations gives $\E_{(I,T)\sim\cD}[\varphi_A^{M'}(I,T)]\ge\E_{(I,T)\sim\cD}[\varphi_A^M(I,T)]\ge c\ge c'$.

\ref{obs:scaling}~Since $U^*(I/\lambda,T)=U^*(I,T)/\lambda$, we have $\E_{(I,T)\sim\cD}[U^*(I/\lambda,T)]\le1$. Let~$A$ be a deterministic online algorithm. As~$A$ need not treat~$I$ and $I/\lambda$ alike, we apply the assumption to the algorithm~$A_\lambda$ that on input~$I$ runs~$A$ on $I/\lambda$ and uses the same rates, that is, $A_\lambda(I)=A(I/\lambda)$. Since $I$ and $I/\lambda$ have the same release times, $A_\lambda$ satisfies the non-anticipation condition. The robust backlogs of~$A$ on $(I/\lambda,T)$ and of~$A_\lambda$ on $(I,T)$ consist of the same jobs up to the scaling of their weights, so $\varphi_A^M(I/\lambda,T)=\varphi_{A_\lambda}^{\lambda M}(I,T)/\lambda$. By assumption, the right-hand side has expectation at least~$c$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Flooding.}
The following lemma turns a gap in unfinished weight at the terminal time into a gap in weighted flow time by releasing a long stream of short, dense jobs. Such streams are used by Becchetti et al.~\cite{BecchettiLMP06} and in the lower bound of Bansal and Chan~\cite{BansalC09}. We need a version for terminal distributions in which the stream does not depend on the algorithm.

\begin{samepage}
\begin{lemma}[Flooding]\label{lem:flooding}
Let $(I,T)$ be a terminal instance, let $M>0$, and let $L$ be a positive multiple of $d=1/(2M)$. Form~$\hat I$ by adding jobs of size~$d$ and weight~$1/2$ at times $T,T+d,\dots,T+L-d$. Then every deterministic online algorithm~$A$ satisfies
\begin{align*}
  F_A(\hat I)&\ge L\,\varphi_A^M(I,T)\,,\\
  \OPT(\hat I)&\le L\bigl(U^*(I,T)+\tfrac12\bigr)+w(I)\bigl(T+p(I)\bigr)\,.
\end{align*}
\end{lemma}
\end{samepage}

\begin{proof}
Call the jobs of~$I$ \emph{old} and the added jobs \emph{stream jobs}.

\emph{Online cost.} Fix~$A$. Since the stream starts at~$T$, non-anticipation implies that the robust backlog at~$T$ is the same in $A(I)$ and $A(\hat I)$. Write $\varphi=\varphi_A^M(I,T)$. For $0\le\theta<L$, let~$y$ be the processing that the old jobs receive during $[T,T+\theta)$. If $y<1$, every job in the robust backlog is still unfinished at time~$T+\theta$, so the unfinished weight at this time is at least~$\varphi$. If $y\ge1$, the stream jobs released by time~$T+\theta$ have total size $d(\lfloor\theta/d\rfloor+1)>\theta$ and receive at most $\theta-y$ processing. Their total remaining size therefore exceeds~$y$. More than $y/d$ stream jobs are unfinished, with total weight greater than $y/(2d)=My\ge M\ge\varphi$. Thus the unfinished weight stays at least~$\varphi$ throughout the stream. Integration gives $F_A(\hat I)\ge L\varphi$.

\emph{Optimal cost.} Until~$T$, follow a schedule that leaves weight $U^*(I,T)$ unfinished. Process every stream job immediately after its release, then finish the remaining old jobs. The old jobs contribute at most $w(I)T$ before~$T$, exactly $L\,U^*(I,T)$ during the stream, and at most $w(I)p(I)$ afterwards. The stream jobs contribute $L/2$. Adding these terms proves the bound.
\end{proof}

The main technical result is the existence of hard terminal distributions with arbitrarily large gaps.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:hard}
For every $\rho\ge1$, there is a $(2\rho,M)$-hard terminal distribution with $M=\lceil4\rho\rceil$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:main}]
Let $\rho\ge1$ and $\gamma\ge0$, and take a $(2\rho,M)$-hard terminal distribution~$\cD$ from~\cref{thm:hard}. Since its support is finite, there is a constant~$B$ such that $w(I)(T+p(I))\le B$ throughout the support. Apply~\cref{lem:flooding} with the same length~$L$ to every terminal instance, where $L$ is a positive multiple of $1/(2M)$ and $L\ge2B+2\gamma/\rho$. For the resulting distribution~$\hat\cD$, hardness and the two bounds of the lemma give
\begin{equation*}
  \E[F_A(\hat I)]-\rho\,\E[\OPT(\hat I)]
  \ge 2\rho L-\rho\bigl(\tfrac32L+B\bigr)
  =\rho(L/2-B)\ge\gamma\,,
\end{equation*}
for every deterministic online algorithm~$A$. This proves the distributional statement. For a randomized algorithm~$\cA$ and any additive constant~$\Gamma\ge0$, \cref{lem:yao} applied to the distribution~$\hat\cD$ for $\gamma=\Gamma+1$ gives an instance~$\hat I$ with $\E_{A\sim\cA}[F_A(\hat I)]\ge \rho\,\OPT(\hat I)+\Gamma+1$. Since $\rho$ and~$\Gamma$ are arbitrary, no randomized online algorithm is $O(1)$-competitive.
\end{proof}

\section{Constructing hard terminal distributions}\label{sec:construction}

We prove~\cref{thm:hard} by alternating two operations on hard terminal distributions. Amplification (\cref{sec:amplification}) multiplies the gap by $1+\eta$ for a small $\eta>0$ that depends on the cap, but it increases the cap. Averaging (\cref{sec:averaging}) reduces the cap to the input gap while losing only a little from that gap. Both operations concatenate independent copies at decreasing time scales.

\subsection{Copying a terminal distribution}\label{sec:copies}

We define the concatenation of copies and show that an offline schedule can handle the copies separately, whereas an online algorithm faces each copy as a fresh sample of the distribution.

Let~$\cD$ be a terminal distribution. A \emph{bound} for~$\cD$ is a number $H\ge1$ such that $T\le H$ and $p(I)\le H$ for every terminal instance $(I,T)$ in its support. The concatenation of~$m$ copies of~$\cD$ places $m$ independent samples of~$\cD$ one after another, stretching the $i$th of them in time and size by a factor~$s_i$. We define these factors backwards, together with numbers~$S_i$ that will bound both the duration and the total size of the later copies $i+1,\dots,m$, and we choose~$s_i$ larger than twice~$S_i$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:concatenation}
Let~$\cD$ be a terminal distribution with a bound~$H$, and let~$m$ be a positive integer. Set $S_m=0$ and, for $i=m,\dots,1$, define $s_i=2S_i+2$ and $S_{i-1}=S_i+Hs_i$. Let $V=S_0$. Let $(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_m,T_m)$ be independent samples from~$\cD$, and set $u_0=0$ and $u_i=u_{i-1}+s_iT_i$ for $i\in[m]$. For each job $j\in I_i$, \emph{copy~$i$} contains a job with release time $u_{i-1}+s_ir_j$, size $s_ip_j$, and weight~$w_j$. The \emph{concatenation of~$m$ copies of~$\cD$} is the union~$\bar I$ of the copies, and $\tau=u_m$ is its \emph{end}.
\end{definition}

Thus copy~$i$ is~$I_i$ stretched in time and size by~$s_i$, started at~$u_{i-1}$, and ending at its terminal time~$u_i$. By construction, for every $i\in[m]$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scales}
  S_i=H\sum_{j=i+1}^m s_j
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  s_i-S_i=S_i+2\,,
\end{equation}
where the first identity also holds for~$i=0$. In particular, $V=S_0$ will bound the duration and the total size of all copies.

An online algorithm~$A$ that processes copy~$i$ has already seen copies $1,\dots,i-1$ and may adapt to them. Nevertheless, copy~$i$ is a fresh sample of~$\cD$, and copies $i+1,\dots,m$ are released only from~$u_i$ on. We measure the robust backlog of~$A$ in copy~$i$ at the scale~$s_i$: it consists of the jobs of copy~$i$ whose remaining size in~$A(\bar I)$ at its terminal time~$u_i$ is at least~$s_i$. After shrinking time and sizes by~$s_i$, these are the jobs with remaining size at least~$1$ at the terminal time~$T_i$, as in the definition of the robust backlog. The scales ensure that the later copies are released during $[u_i,\tau)$, an interval of length at most~$S_i$, so a job receives at most~$S_i$ processing during this interval. Hence the robust backlog of copy~$i$ survives the later copies: its jobs are still unfinished at~$\tau$, with remaining size at least $s_i-S_i=S_i+2$ (\cref{fig:copies}).


\begin{lemma}\label{lem:copies}
Let~$\cD$ be a $(c,M)$-hard terminal distribution, and let~$\bar I$ be the concatenation of $m$ copies formed from independent samples $(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_m,T_m)$ from~$\cD$, with end~$\tau$, as in~\cref{def:concatenation}.
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item\label{it:geometry} The jobs of copy~$i$ are released during $[u_{i-1},u_i)$. For every $i\in\{0,\dots,m\}$, copies $i+1,\dots,m$ have total size at most~$S_i$, and $\tau-u_i\le S_i$. In particular, $p(\bar I)\le V$ and $\tau\le V$.
\item\label{it:offline} There is a schedule of~$\bar I$ in which, for every $i\in[m]$, the jobs of copy~$i$ that are unfinished at time~$\tau$ have total weight $B_i=U^*(I_i,T_i)$. Moreover, $\E_{(I_i,T_i)\sim\cD}[B_i]\le1$.
\item\label{it:online} Let~$A$ be a deterministic online algorithm. For $i\in[m]$, let~$E_i$ be the set of jobs of copy~$i$ whose remaining size at time~$u_i$ in $A(\bar I)$ is at least~$s_i$, and let $X_i=\min\{M,w(E_i)\}$. Then $X_i$ is determined by $(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_i,T_i)$. For every realization of the preceding $i-1$ samples of positive probability,
\begin{equation*}
  \E_{(I_i,T_i)\sim\cD}\bigl[X_i\bigm|(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_{i-1},T_{i-1})\bigr]\ge c\,.
\end{equation*}
Moreover, every job of~$E_i$ has remaining size at least $S_i+2$ at time~$\tau$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
\ref{it:geometry}~A job of copy~$i$ is released at time $u_{i-1}+s_ir_j$ with $0\le r_j<T_i$, hence during $[u_{i-1},u_i)$. For each $i'\in[m]$, copy~$i'$ has total size $s_{i'}\,p(I_{i'})\le Hs_{i'}$, and $u_{i'}-u_{i'-1}=s_{i'}T_{i'}\le Hs_{i'}$. Summing over $i'>i$ and using~\eqref{eq:scales}, we find that copies $i+1,\dots,m$ have total size at most~$S_i$ and that $\tau-u_i\le S_i$. The case $i=0$ gives $p(\bar I)\le V$ and $\tau\le V$.

\ref{it:offline}~During $[u_{i-1},u_i)$, process copy~$i$ by a schedule of~$I_i$ that leaves weight $B_i=U^*(I_i,T_i)$ unfinished at~$T_i$, stretched in time and size by~$s_i$, and do not process copy~$i$ afterwards. Stretching preserves feasibility, and the time windows are disjoint, so these schedules combine into a schedule of~$\bar I$ that leaves weight~$B_i$ of each copy~$i$ unfinished at~$\tau$. Since $(I_i,T_i)$ is a sample from the $(c,M)$-hard distribution~$\cD$, we have $\E_{(I_i,T_i)\sim\cD}[B_i]=\E_{(I_i,T_i)\sim\cD}[U^*(I_i,T_i)]\le1$ by~\cref{def:hard}\ref{def:hard-offline}.

\ref{it:online}~Fix $i\in[m]$ and a realization of the first $i-1$ samples $(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_{i-1},T_{i-1})$ of positive probability. This fixes copies $1,\dots,i-1$, their union~$\bar I_{<i}$, and the time~$u_{i-1}$ (\cref{fig:copies}). The idea is that, with these copies fixed, the way in which~$A$ processes copy~$i$ is a deterministic online algorithm~$A_i$ for the unstretched sample~$I_i$. Since~$(I_i,T_i)$ is a fresh sample from~$\cD$, the hardness of~$\cD$ applies to~$A_i$.

To define~$A_i$, let $\sigma_i(j)=(u_{i-1}+s_ir_j,\,s_ip_j,\,w_j)$ for a job $j=(r_j,p_j,w_j)$, and let $\sigma_i(I)=\{\sigma_i(j):j\in I\}$ for an instance~$I$. Thus copy~$i$ is~$\sigma_i(I_i)$. On input~$I$, algorithm~$A_i$ runs~$A$ on $\bar I_{<i}\cup\sigma_i(I)$ and, at time~$t$, gives each job $j\in I$ the rate that~$A$ gives~$\sigma_i(j)$ at time $u_{i-1}+s_it$. Processing that~$A$ assigns to~$\bar I_{<i}$ becomes idle time for~$A_i$. Since time and sizes are stretched by the same factor~$s_i$, these rates form a schedule of~$I$, and the remaining size of~$j$ at time~$t$ in $A_i(I)$ is the remaining size of~$\sigma_i(j)$ at time $u_{i-1}+s_it$ in $A(\bar I_{<i}\cup\sigma_i(I))$, divided by~$s_i$. Moreover, $A_i$ is a deterministic online algorithm: if two inputs contain the same jobs with release times before~$t$, then the instances on which~$A$ runs contain the same jobs with release times before $u_{i-1}+s_it$, so~$A_i$ inherits non-anticipation from~$A$.

Now consider the input~$I_i$. The instance $\bar I_{<i}\cup\sigma_i(I_i)$ consists of copies $1,\dots,i$. It contains the same jobs as~$\bar I$ with release times before~$u_i$, because copies $i+1,\dots,m$ are released at~$u_i$ or later by~\ref{it:geometry}. By non-anticipation, the jobs of copy~$i$ therefore have the same remaining sizes at time~$u_i$ in $A(\bar I)$ and in $A(\bar I_{<i}\cup\sigma_i(I_i))$. Since $u_i=u_{i-1}+s_iT_i$, a job~$\sigma_i(j)$ of copy~$i$ belongs to~$E_i$ if and only if~$j$ has remaining size at least~$1$ at time~$T_i$ in $A_i(I_i)$, that is, if and only if $j\in R_{A_i}(I_i,T_i)$. As~$\sigma_i$ preserves weights, we obtain $X_i=\varphi_{A_i}^M(I_i,T_i)$. Since~$A_i$ depends only on the first $i-1$ samples, $X_i$ is determined by the first~$i$ samples.

Since~$A_i$ does not depend on~$(I_i,T_i)$ and the samples are independent, $X_i$ is the capped robust backlog of a fixed algorithm on a fresh sample from~$\cD$. As~$\cD$ is $(c,M)$-hard, applying~\cref{def:hard}\ref{def:hard-online} to~$A_i$ yields
\begin{equation*}
  \E_{(I_i,T_i)\sim\cD}\bigl[X_i\bigm|(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_{i-1},T_{i-1})\bigr]
  =\E_{(I,T)\sim\cD}\bigl[\varphi_{A_i}^M(I,T)\bigr]
  \ge c\,.
\end{equation*}

Finally, a job of~$E_i$ has remaining size at least~$s_i$ at~$u_i$ and receives at most $\tau-u_i\le S_i$ processing before~$\tau$. Its remaining size at~$\tau$ is therefore at least $s_i-S_i=S_i+2$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Amplification}\label{sec:amplification}

Amplification appends a heavy job to $k$ copies of a terminal distribution and hides the terminal time among $k$ checkpoints after the release of this job. Let $M\ge1$ be an integer and $k=2M+1$. Let~$\cD$ be a terminal distribution with a bound~$H$, and let~$\bar I$ be the concatenation of~$k$ copies of~$\cD$ (\cref{def:concatenation}), with the notation of~\cref{sec:copies} for $m=k$. We add a job~$J$ with release time~$\tau$, size~$V$, and weight~$2M$, and we define for each $i\in[k]$ the \emph{checkpoint} $h_i=\tau+V+S_i$. Since $S_1>S_2>\dots>S_k=0$, we have $h_1>h_2>\dots>h_k=\tau+V$. By~\cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:geometry}, copies $i+1,\dots,k$ have total size at most~$S_i$, and all copies together have total size at most~$V$. Hence the time $V+S_i$ from~$\tau$ to~$h_i$ suffices to complete either~$J$ and copies $i+1,\dots,k$, or all copies.

The terminal time is the checkpoint~$h_K$ for a random index $K\in[k]$, drawn independently of the copies with $\Pr[K\ge i]=2^{1-i}$ for all $i\in[k]$. Equivalently, $K$ counts fair coin flips up to and including the first head, truncated at~$k$. Thus $2\Pr[K=i]\ge\Pr[K\ge i]$ for all $i\in[k]$, with equality for $i<k$. Moreover, $\E[K]=\sum_{i=1}^k\Pr[K\ge i]=2-2^{1-k}$ and $\Pr[K=k]=2^{1-k}$. Set $\beta=\E[K]-\Pr[K=k]=2-2^{2-k}$. The \emph{amplified} terminal distribution $\Amp_M(\cD)$ is the distribution of $\bigl((\bar I\cup\{J\})/\beta,h_K\bigr)$.

Suppose that~$\cD$ is $(c,M)$-hard, and consider the weights before they are divided by~$\beta$. At~$h_i$, an offline schedule can leave unfinished either jobs of copies $1,\dots,i$, of expected total weight at most~$i$ by~\cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:offline}, or only~$J$, of weight $2M=k-1$. The second option is better only at the earliest checkpoint~$h_k$, so the expected minimum unfinished weight at~$h_K$ is at most $\E[K]-\Pr[K=k]=\beta$. Online, at each checkpoint~$h_j$, either~$J$ is robust, or all jobs in the robust backlog~$E_j$ of copy~$j$ are still robust at~$h_j$ and at all earlier checkpoints. As the weight~$2M$ of~$J$ is twice the cap and $2\Pr[K=j]\ge\Pr[K\ge j]$, each copy~$j$ accounts for expected capped robust backlog at least $c\Pr[K\ge j]$, for a total of at least $c\,\E[K]$ (\cref{fig:amp-accounting}). Dividing all weights by~$\beta$ therefore multiplies the gap by $\E[K]/\beta>1$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:amplification}
Let $M\ge1$ be an integer, $k=2M+1$, and $\eta=2^{-k}$. If~$\cD$ is $(c,M)$-hard, then $\Amp_M(\cD)$ is $((1+\eta)c,(k+1)M)$-hard. Moreover, every terminal instance $(I,T)$ in its support satisfies $U^*(I,T)\le2M$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
All jobs of $\bar I\cup\{J\}$ are released by time $\tau<h_K$, and the support is finite, so $\Amp_M(\cD)$ is a terminal distribution. We show that, before the weights are divided by~$\beta$,
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:amp-goal}
\begin{align}
  \E\bigl[U^*(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)\bigr]&\le\beta\,,\label{eq:amp-goal-offline}\\*
  \E\bigl[\varphi_A^{(k+1)M}(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)\bigr]&\ge c\,\E[K]\label{eq:amp-goal-online}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
for every deterministic online algorithm~$A$, where the expectations are over the copies and~$K$.

We first derive the lemma from~\eqref{eq:amp-goal}. Since $k\ge3$, we have $1\le\beta\le2$. As $\E[K]=\beta+2^{1-k}$, it follows that
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{\E[K]}{\beta}=1+\frac{2^{1-k}}{\beta}\ge1+2^{-k}=1+\eta\,.
\end{equation*}
Raising the cap in~\eqref{eq:amp-goal-online} from $(k+1)M$ to $\beta(k+1)M$ can only increase the capped robust backlog, so
\begin{equation*}
  \E\bigl[\varphi_A^{\beta(k+1)M}(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)\bigr]\ge c\,\E[K]\ge\beta(1+\eta)c\,.
\end{equation*}
With~\eqref{eq:amp-goal-offline}, this verifies the assumptions of~\cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:scaling} for the distribution of $(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)$, with $\lambda=\beta$, gap $(1+\eta)c$, and cap $(k+1)M$; note that $(1+\eta)c\le2M\le(k+1)M$ because $c\le M$. Hence $\Amp_M(\cD)$, the distribution of $\bigl((\bar I\cup\{J\})/\beta,h_K\bigr)$, is $((1+\eta)c,(k+1)M)$-hard.

\emph{Proof of~\eqref{eq:amp-goal-offline}.} Follow the schedule of~\cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:offline} until~$\tau$; it leaves weight~$B_j$ of copy~$j$ unfinished at~$\tau$, where $\E[B_j]\le1$. By the choice of~$h_i$, it can then complete during $[\tau,h_i)$ either~$J$ and the remaining jobs of copies $i+1,\dots,k$, or all remaining jobs of~$\bar I$. Thus
\begin{equation*}
  U^*(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_i)\le\min\left\{\sum_{j\le i}B_j,\,2M\right\}\,.
\end{equation*}
In particular, $U^*(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)\le2M$ for every realization of the copies and~$K$. Dividing the weights by~$\beta\ge1$ gives $U^*(I,T)\le2M$ for every terminal instance $(I,T)$ in the support of~$\Amp_M(\cD)$, which is the second claim of the lemma.

To bound the expectation, fix $i\in[k]$. Since~$K$ is independent of the copies, $\E[B_j\mid K=i]=\E[B_j]\le1$ for every~$j$. As the minimum is at most each of its arguments, taking conditional expectations yields
\begin{equation*}
  \E\bigl[U^*(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)\bigm|K=i\bigr]\le\min\left\{\sum_{j\le i}\E[B_j\mid K=i],\,2M\right\}\le\min\{i,2M\}\,.
\end{equation*}
Averaging over~$K$ and using $2M=k-1$ gives
\begin{equation*}
  \E\bigl[U^*(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)\bigr]\le\E[\min\{K,k-1\}]=\E[K]-\Pr[K=k]=\beta\,.
\end{equation*}


\emph{Proof of~\eqref{eq:amp-goal-online}.} Fix a deterministic online algorithm~$A$. Write $\mathbf Z=((I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_k,T_k))\sim\cD^k$ for the tuple of independent samples that form the copies, and fix a realization of~$\mathbf Z$; we average over~$\mathbf Z$ only in the last step. The variable~$K$ affects only the terminal time, so the schedule $A(\bar I\cup\{J\})$ is fixed, and only the checkpoint at which we evaluate it is random. Let~$E_j$ and~$X_j$ be as in~\cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:online}. Since~$J$ is released at~$\tau$, non-anticipation implies that $A(\bar I\cup\{J\})$ and $A(\bar I)$ process the jobs of~$\bar I$ identically before~$\tau$. Hence every job of~$E_j$ has remaining size at least $S_j+2$ at~$\tau$ also in $A(\bar I\cup\{J\})$.

We first show that for each $j\in[k]$, either~$J$ is robust at~$h_j$, or all jobs of~$E_j$ are robust at every checkpoint~$h_i$ with $i\ge j$. Suppose that~$J$ is not robust at~$h_j$. Then~$J$ received more than $V-1$ processing during $[\tau,h_j)$, which leaves less than $(V+S_j)-(V-1)=S_j+1$ processing for the jobs of~$\bar I$. Every job of~$E_j$ therefore has remaining size greater than~$1$ at~$h_j$, and hence also at every earlier checkpoint~$h_i$ with $i\ge j$.

We now bound the capped robust backlog from below by charges to the copies. For each copy~$j \in [k]$, assign charges according to the following two cases (\cref{fig:amp-accounting}):
\begin{itemize}
\item If~$J$ is not robust at~$h_j$, charge~$X_j$ at every checkpoint~$h_i$ with $i\ge j$.
\item If~$J$ is robust at~$h_j$, charge~$2M$ at~$h_j$ only.
\end{itemize}


At a fixed checkpoint~$h_i$, only copies $j\le i$ are charged. Their total charge satisfies two bounds:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Robust backlog.} A charge~$X_j$ is at most~$w(E_j)$, and the jobs of~$E_j$ are then robust at~$h_i$. A charge~$2M$ comes only from copy~$i$ and equals the weight of~$J$, which is then robust at~$h_i$. Since the sets $E_1,\dots,E_k$ and~$\{J\}$ are pairwise disjoint, the total charge is at most the weight of the robust backlog at~$h_i$.
\item \emph{Cap.} If copy~$i$ receives charge~$2M$, the at most $k-1$ other charged copies receive at most~$M$ each, so the total is at most $2M+(k-1)M=(k+1)M$. Otherwise, the total is at most~$kM$.
\end{itemize}
Thus the total charge at~$h_i$ is at most $\varphi_A^{(k+1)M}(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_i)$.

Finally, we take expectations, first over~$K$ with~$\mathbf Z$ fixed. The copies, the schedule, and the values~$X_j$ then stay fixed, and the distribution of~$K$ is unchanged because~$K$ is independent of~$\mathbf Z$. The expected charge of copy~$j$ at~$h_K$ is $X_j\Pr[K\ge j]$ if~$J$ is not robust at~$h_j$, and $2M\Pr[K=j]$ otherwise. Since $X_j\le M$ and $2\Pr[K=j]\ge\Pr[K\ge j]$, both are at least $X_j\Pr[K\ge j]$. As the total charge at~$h_K$ is at most $\varphi_A^{(k+1)M}(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)$ for every value of~$K$, summing over the copies gives
\begin{equation*}
  \E_K\bigl[\varphi_A^{(k+1)M}(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)\bigm|\mathbf Z\bigr]\ge\sum_{j=1}^k\Pr[K\ge j]\,X_j\,.
\end{equation*}
We now average over~$\mathbf Z\sim\cD^k$. Since~$X_j$ is determined by the first~$j$ samples, averaging the conditional bound of~\cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:online} over the first $j-1$ samples gives $\E_{\mathbf Z\sim\cD^k}[X_j]\ge c$ for every~$j$. The law of total expectation therefore yields
\begin{align*}
  \E_{\mathbf Z,K}\bigl[\varphi_A^{(k+1)M}(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)\bigr]
  &=\E_{\mathbf Z\sim\cD^k}\Bigl[\E_K\bigl[\varphi_A^{(k+1)M}(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)\bigm|\mathbf Z\bigr]\Bigr]\\*
  &\ge\sum_{j=1}^k\Pr[K\ge j]\,\E_{\mathbf Z\sim\cD^k}[X_j]\\*
  &\ge c\sum_{j=1}^k\Pr[K\ge j]=c\,\E_K[K]\,.
\end{align*}
This is~\eqref{eq:amp-goal-online}.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:amplification}
Apply amplification with $M=1$ to the $(1,1)$-hard single-job distribution of~\cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:base}. Then $k=3$, and~$K$ takes the values $1,2,3$ with probabilities $1/2,1/4,1/4$. Each copy leaves weight $B_j=1$ unfinished offline, so the minimum unfinished weight at $h_1,h_2,h_3$ is at most $1$, $2$, and~$2$, respectively, and at most $3/2=\beta$ in expectation. The charging argument gives expected capped robust backlog at least $\E[K]=7/4$. After normalization, the gap is therefore at least $7/6$, more than the gap $(1+\eta)c=9/8$ guaranteed by~\cref{lem:amplification}. The gain comes from offline saving one unit at the earliest checkpoint~$h_3$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Averaging}\label{sec:averaging}

For a terminal distribution~$\cD$ with a bound~$H$ and a positive integer~$m$, the \emph{averaged} terminal distribution $\Avg_m(\cD)$ is the distribution of the terminal instance $(\bar I/m,\tau)$, where~$\bar I$ is the concatenation of~$m$ copies of~$\cD$ (\cref{def:concatenation}), formed with the bound~$H$, and~$\tau$ is its end.

Suppose that~$\cD$ is $(c,M)$-hard. Offline, the copies can be handled separately, so the expected minimum unfinished weight at~$\tau$ remains at most~$1$ after the weights are divided by~$m$. Online, the robust backlog at~$\tau$ contains the robust backlogs~$E_i$ of all copies. After the division, its weight is therefore at least the average of their capped weights $X_1,\dots,X_m\in[0,M]$. These need not be independent, since the algorithm adapts to earlier copies, but each has conditional expectation at least~$c$ given the earlier copies. A second-moment bound shows that capping their average at~$c$ loses at most $M/\sqrt m$ in expectation.


\begin{lemma}\label{lem:averaging}
Let~$\cD$ be a $(c,M)$-hard terminal distribution and let~$m$ be a positive integer. For $(I,T)\sim\Avg_m(\cD)$, we have
\begin{equation*}
  \E[U^*(I,T)]\le1
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \E[\varphi_A^c(I,T)]\ge c-\frac{M}{\sqrt m}
\end{equation*}
for every deterministic online algorithm~$A$.
\end{lemma}

In particular, if $c>M/\sqrt m$, then $\Avg_m(\cD)$ is $(c-M/\sqrt m,c)$-hard.

\begin{proof}
All jobs of~$\bar I$ are released before~$\tau$ by~\cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:geometry}, so $\Avg_m(\cD)$ is a terminal distribution. Dividing all weights by~$m$ divides~$U^*$ by~$m$. Moreover, for a deterministic online algorithm~$A$, the algorithm~$A'$ with $A'(I)=A(I/m)$ is a deterministic online algorithm with $\varphi_{A'}^{cm}(\bar I,\tau)=m\,\varphi_A^c(\bar I/m,\tau)$, as in the proof of~\cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:scaling}. It therefore suffices to show that
\begin{equation*}
  \E[U^*(\bar I,\tau)]\le m
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \E[\varphi_A^{cm}(\bar I,\tau)]\ge cm-M\sqrt m
\end{equation*}
for every deterministic online algorithm~$A$. The offline bound holds because \cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:offline} gives $U^*(\bar I,\tau)\le B_1+\dots+B_m$ with $\E[B_i]\le1$.

Fix~$A$, and let~$E_i$ and~$X_i$ be as in~\cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:online}. Every job of~$E_i$ has remaining size at least $S_i+2\ge2$ at~$\tau$, so the robust backlog at~$\tau$ contains the pairwise disjoint sets $E_1,\dots,E_m$. Since $X_i\le w(E_i)$, we obtain
\begin{equation*}
  \varphi_A^{cm}(\bar I,\tau)\ge\min\left\{cm,\sum_{i=1}^mX_i\right\}=cm-\Delta_m\,,
\end{equation*}
where $\Delta_m=\bigl(cm-\sum_{i=1}^mX_i\bigr)^+$. It remains to show that $\E[\Delta_m]\le M\sqrt m$.

We bound~$\E[\Delta_m^2]$ by induction over the copies. For $i=0,\dots,m$, let $\Delta_i=\bigl(ic-\sum_{j=1}^iX_j\bigr)^+$. We show that $\E[\Delta_i^2]\le iM^2$ for all~$i$. This holds for $i=0$, since $\Delta_0=0$. Now let $i\in[m]$. Since $(i-1)c-\sum_{j<i}X_j\le \Delta_{i-1}$ and $x\mapsto x^+$ is nondecreasing, we have $\Delta_i\le(\Delta_{i-1}+c-X_i)^+$. Squaring and using $0\le X_i\le M$ and $c\le M$, we obtain
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta_i^2\le(\Delta_{i-1}+c-X_i)^2\le \Delta_{i-1}^2+2\Delta_{i-1}(c-X_i)+M^2\,.
\end{equation*}
Fix a realization of the first $i-1$ samples of positive probability. By~\cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:online}, these samples determine $X_1,\dots,X_{i-1}$ and hence the value $\Delta_{i-1}\ge0$. By the same lemma, the conditional expectation of~$X_i$ given these samples is at least~$c$. Therefore
\begin{equation*}
  \E\bigl[2\Delta_{i-1}(c-X_i)\bigm|(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_{i-1},T_{i-1})\bigr]
  =2\Delta_{i-1}\Bigl(c-\E\bigl[X_i\bigm|(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_{i-1},T_{i-1})\bigr]\Bigr)\le0\,.
\end{equation*}
By the law of total expectation, the cross term $2\Delta_{i-1}(c-X_i)$ has nonpositive expectation. Taking expectations in the bound on~$\Delta_i^2$ gives $\E[\Delta_i^2]\le\E[\Delta_{i-1}^2]+M^2\le iM^2$ by the induction hypothesis. For $i=m$, Cauchy--Schwarz gives $\E[\Delta_m]\le\sqrt{\E[\Delta_m^2]}\le M\sqrt m$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of \texorpdfstring{\cref{thm:hard}}{Theorem 3.4}}\label{sec:iteration}


Fix $\rho\ge1$ and let $M=\lceil4\rho\rceil$. This will be the cap of the distributions~$\cD_q$ constructed below, and the last of them will have gap $M/2\ge2\rho$. Set
\begin{equation*}
  k=2M+1,\qquad \eta=2^{-k},\qquad
  m=\left(\frac{2(k+1)M}{\eta}\right)^2,\qquad
  N=\frac{M-2}{\eta}\,.
\end{equation*}
Here~$k$ and~$\eta$ are the parameters of~\cref{lem:amplification}, and~$m$ is chosen so that averaging an input with cap $(k+1)M$ loses at most $(k+1)M/\sqrt m=\eta/2$ by~\cref{lem:averaging}. Since $M\ge4$ and $1/\eta=2^k$, both~$m$ and~$N$ are positive integers. Let~$\cD_0$ be the single-job distribution of~\cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:base}, and for $q=1,\dots,N$ let
\begin{equation*}
  \cD_q=\Avg_m(\Amp_M(\cD_{q-1}))\,.
\end{equation*}
We call one amplification followed by one averaging a \emph{round}, so~$N$ is the number of rounds. Both operations depend on a bound for their input distribution through the scales of~\cref{def:concatenation}. \Cref{lem:amplification,lem:averaging} hold for any bound, and we fix specific bounds in~\cref{sec:quantitative}.

We show by induction on~$q$ that~$\cD_q$ is $(c_q,M)$-hard, where $c_q=1+q\eta/2$. For $q=0$, this is~\cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:base}. Let $q\in[N]$, and suppose that~$\cD_{q-1}$ is $(c_{q-1},M)$-hard. By~\cref{lem:amplification}, amplification gives gap $(1+\eta)c_{q-1}$ and cap $(k+1)M$. By~\cref{lem:averaging}, averaging then loses at most $(k+1)M/\sqrt m=\eta/2$ from the gap and reduces the cap to $(1+\eta)c_{q-1}$. Since $c_{q-1}\ge1$, the gap of~$\cD_q$ is at least
\begin{equation*}
  (1+\eta)c_{q-1}-\eta/2\ge c_{q-1}+\eta/2=c_q\,.
\end{equation*}
Its cap satisfies $(1+\eta)c_{q-1}<M$, because $c_{q-1}<c_N=M/2$ and $\eta<1$. By~\cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:monotone}, $\cD_q$ is $(c_q,M)$-hard. Finally, $c_N=M/2\ge2\rho$, which proves~\cref{thm:hard}.

By induction on~$q$, all instances in the support of~$\cD_q$ have the same number of jobs: if this number is~$n$ for~$\cD_{q-1}$, then it is $m(kn+1)$ for~$\cD_q$. Flooding (\cref{lem:flooding}) with a common stream length adds the same number of jobs to each instance. Hence our lower bounds hold even for algorithms that know the number of jobs in advance.

\section{Dependence on \texorpdfstring{$W$, $P$, $D$, and~$n$}{W, P, D, and n}}\label{sec:quantitative}

For an instance~$I$, let $W(I)=\max_{j\in I}w_j/\min_{j\in I}w_j$ and $P(I)=\max_{j\in I}p_j/\min_{j\in I}p_j$ be its weight and size ratios. Let $D(I)=\max_{j\in I}(w_j/p_j)/\min_{j\in I}(w_j/p_j)$ be its density ratio, and let~$|I|$ be its number of jobs. In this section, all logarithms are binary. We analyze the construction of \cref{sec:iteration} and prove the following refinement of \cref{thm:main}.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:quant-refined}
There is an absolute constant $C\ge1$ such that for all $\rho\ge1$ and $\gamma\ge0$, there is a probability distribution~$\hat\cD$ over finitely many instances such that every instance~$\hat I$ in its support satisfies
\begin{equation*}
  W(\hat I)\le2^{2^{C\rho}},\qquad \max\{P(\hat I),D(\hat I),|\hat I|\}\le2^{2^{2^{C\rho}}}\,,
\end{equation*}
and every deterministic online algorithm~$A$ satisfies $\E_{\hat I\sim\hat\cD}[F_A(\hat I)]\ge \rho\cdot\E_{\hat I\sim\hat\cD}[\OPT(\hat I)]+\gamma$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
It suffices to prove the claim for $\gamma=1$. Multiplying all weights by $\lambda=\max\{1,\gamma\}$ preserves $W$, $P$, $D$, and the number of jobs, and it scales both costs by~$\lambda$. For each algorithm~$A$ on the scaled instances, apply the guarantee for $\gamma=1$ to $A'(I)=A(\lambda I)$, as in the weight-scaling argument of~\cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:scaling}.

Let $M$, $k$, $\eta$, $m$, $N$, and $\cD_0,\dots,\cD_N$ be as in \cref{sec:iteration}. We obtain~$\hat\cD$ by flooding~$\cD_N$ (\cref{lem:flooding}) with a common stream length~$L$ chosen below. The stream jobs have weight~$1/2$ and size $1/(2M)$.

\emph{Weights.} All weights in~$\cD_q$ lie in $[(2m)^{-q},1]$. Indeed, the initial weight is~$1$. Each round adds a heavy job of weight~$2M$ and divides all weights by~$\beta m\in[m,2m]$, where $\beta\in[1,2]$ is the normalization of amplification. The claim follows by induction, since $m\ge2M$. The stream weight~$1/2$ lies in the same interval for $q=N\ge1$, so $W(\hat I)\le(2m)^N$.

\emph{Sizes.} The backward recurrence for the copy scales in~\cref{def:concatenation} gives $S_{i-1}+1=(2H+1)(S_i+1)$. Since $S_r=0$ for a concatenation of~$r$ copies, and $2H+1\le H^2$ for $H\ge3$, such a concatenation with bound $H\ge3$ has
\begin{equation*}
  V=(2H+1)^r-1\le H^{2r}\,.
\end{equation*}
The output of amplification with bound~$H$ has total size at most~$2V$ and terminal times at most $\tau+V+S_1\le3V$, so $H^{3k}$ is a bound for it, because $3V\le3H^{2k}\le H^{3k}$. Averaging with the bound~$H^{3k}$ then yields total sizes and terminal times at most $(H^{3k})^{2m}=H^{6km}$, by~\cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:geometry}. We therefore fix the bounds as follows: in round~$q$, amplification uses the bound~$H_{q-1}$, and averaging uses the bound~$H_{q-1}^{3k}$, where
\begin{equation*}
  H_0=3,\qquad H_q=H_{q-1}^{6km}=3^{(6km)^q}\,.
\end{equation*}
By induction, $H_q$ is a bound for~$\cD_q$. All jobs in~$\cD_q$ also have size at least~$2$: the initial job has size~$2$, copying stretches sizes by factors $s_i\ge2$, and every heavy job has size $V\ge2H\ge2$. After flooding, all sizes lie in $[1/(2M),H_N]$, so $P(\hat I)\le2MH_N$.

\emph{Densities.} For every instance~$I$, the ratio of any two job densities satisfies $(w_j/p_j)/(w_\ell/p_\ell)=(w_j/w_\ell)(p_\ell/p_j)\le W(I)P(I)$. Hence $D(I)\le W(I)P(I)$, and the preceding bounds give $D(\hat I)\le2MH_N(2m)^N$.

\emph{Cost and number of jobs.} An instance~$I$ in the support of~$\cD_N$ consists of jobs of size at least~$2$ and has total size at most~$H_N$, so it has at most $H_N/2$ jobs, each of weight at most~$1$. Hence $w(I)\le H_N$, and the additive term $w(I)(T+p(I))$ in~\cref{lem:flooding} is at most $2H_N^2$. Choose $L=4H_N^2+2$, a multiple of $1/(2M)$ because~$H_N$ and~$M$ are integers. Since~$\cD_N$ is $(2\rho,M)$-hard, the lemma gives
\begin{equation*}
  \E[F_A(\hat I)]-\rho\,\E[\OPT(\hat I)]
  \ge2\rho L-\rho\bigl(\tfrac32L+2H_N^2\bigr)
  =\rho(L/2-2H_N^2)=\rho\ge1
\end{equation*}
for every deterministic online algorithm~$A$, as required. The stream adds $2ML=2M(4H_N^2+2)$ jobs. As $M\ge4$ and $H_N\ge2$, we obtain $|\hat I|\le H_N/2+2M(4H_N^2+2)\le(2MH_N)^2$.

\emph{Growth in~$\rho$.} We have $M\le5\rho$ and $k\le11\rho$. The explicit parameters of the iteration give
\begin{equation*}
  \log m=2k+2\log(2(k+1)M)=O(\rho),\qquad
  N=(M-2)2^k=2^{O(\rho)}\,.
\end{equation*}
Consequently,
\begin{equation*}
  \log W(\hat I)\le N\log(2m)=2^{O(\rho)},\qquad
  \log\log H_N=N\log(6km)+O(1)=2^{O(\rho)}\,.
\end{equation*}
Together with $P(\hat I)\le2MH_N$, $D(\hat I)\le2MH_N(2m)^N$, and $|\hat I|\le(2MH_N)^2$, these estimates give the claimed bounds with an absolute constant~$C$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:quant}]
Let $W,P,D,n\ge16$, and let~$C$ be the constant of~\cref{thm:quant-refined}. Fix a randomized online algorithm~$\cA$ and set $\rho=\min\{\log\log W,\log\log\log\min\{P,D,n\}\}/C$. This is positive because $W,P,D,n\ge16$. If $\rho<1$, there is nothing to show, since no algorithm has competitive ratio less than~$1$.

Otherwise, let $\Gamma\ge0$. By~\cref{thm:quant-refined} with $\gamma=\Gamma+1$ and~\cref{lem:yao}, there is an instance~$\hat I$ satisfying
\begin{equation*}
  W(\hat I)\le2^{2^{C\rho}}\le W,\qquad
  \max\{P(\hat I),D(\hat I),|\hat I|\}\le2^{2^{2^{C\rho}}}\le\min\{P,D,n\}
\end{equation*}
and $\E_{A\sim\cA}[F_A(\hat I)]\ge \rho\cdot\OPT(\hat I)+\Gamma+1$. Hence~$\cA$ is not $\rho$-competitive on this class of instances, proving the claimed lower bound.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A high-probability lower bound}\label{sec:probability}

The last averaging step of the construction also gives a lower bound that holds with high probability.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:probability}
There is an absolute constant $C\ge1$ such that, for every $\rho\ge1$, $\gamma\ge0$, and randomized online algorithm~$\cA$, there is an instance~$I$ with
\begin{equation*}
  W(I)\le2^{2^{C\rho}},\qquad
  \max\{P(I),D(I),|I|\}\le2^{2^{2^{C\rho}}}
\end{equation*}
and
\begin{equation*}
  \Pr_{A\sim\cA}\bigl[F_A(I)\ge \rho\,\OPT(I)+\gamma\bigr]\ge1-2^{-\rho}\,.
\end{equation*}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Use the construction of~\cref{sec:iteration} with~$2\rho$ in place of~$\rho$. Thus $M=\lceil8\rho\rceil$, $k=2M+1$, $\eta=2^{-k}$, and $m=(2(k+1)M/\eta)^2$, and~$\cD_N$ is obtained by averaging $\Amp_M(\cD_{N-1})$. This amplified distribution has gap $(1+\eta)c_{N-1}\ge c_N=M/2$ and cap $(k+1)M$, so it is $(M/2,(k+1)M)$-hard by~\cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:monotone}. By~\cref{lem:averaging}, $(I,T)\sim\cD_N$ therefore satisfies
\begin{equation*}
  \E[\varphi_A^{M/2}(I,T)]\ge M/2-\eta/2
\end{equation*}
for every deterministic online algorithm~$A$. Since $\varphi_A^M\ge\varphi_A^{M/2}$, $0\le\varphi_A^{M/2}\le M/2$, and $M/2\ge4\rho$, Markov's inequality applied to $M/2-\varphi_A^{M/2}(I,T)$ yields
\begin{equation*}
  \Pr\bigl[\varphi_A^M(I,T)<3\rho\bigr]
  \le\Pr\bigl[M/2-\varphi_A^{M/2}(I,T)>M/2-3\rho\bigr]
  \le\frac{\eta}{2\rho}\,.
\end{equation*}

For the offline side, let $B_1,\dots,B_m$ be the values of~$U^*$ on the independent amplified samples entering the last averaging step. By~\cref{lem:amplification}, $0\le B_i\le2M$ and $\E[B_i]\le1$, so $\operatorname{Var}(B_i)\le\E[B_i^2]\le2M$. The offline schedule of~\cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:offline}, with weights divided by~$m$, gives $U^*(I,T)\le m^{-1}\sum_iB_i$. Independence and Chebyshev's inequality therefore imply
\begin{equation*}
  \Pr\bigl[U^*(I,T)>2\bigr]\le\frac{2M}{m}\,.
\end{equation*}
Since $\eta/(2\rho)\le\eta/2$ and $2M/m=\eta^2/(2(k+1)^2M)\le\eta/2$, every deterministic~$A$ satisfies
\begin{equation*}
  \varphi_A^M(I,T)\ge3\rho
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  U^*(I,T)\le2
\end{equation*}
with probability at least $1-\eta\ge1-2^{-\rho}$, where the last inequality uses $k\ge\rho$.

Flood~$\cD_N$ with the stream length $L=4H_N^2+2$ from the proof of~\cref{thm:quant-refined}, applied to the construction with~$2\rho$. On the event above, the pointwise bounds of~\cref{lem:flooding} give
\begin{equation*}
  F_A(\hat I)-\rho\,\OPT(\hat I)
  \ge3\rho L-\rho\bigl(\tfrac52L+2H_N^2\bigr)
  =\rho(L/2-2H_N^2)=\rho\ge1\,.
\end{equation*}
Thus the resulting finite distribution~$\hat\cD$ satisfies $\Pr_{\hat I\sim\hat\cD}[F_A(\hat I)\ge \rho\,\OPT(\hat I)+1]\ge1-2^{-\rho}$ for every deterministic~$A$. The parameter bounds follow from the proof of~\cref{thm:quant-refined}, with an absolute constant enlarged to account for the use of~$2\rho$. Scaling all weights as in its proof replaces the additive term~$1$ by any~$\gamma\ge0$, preserving the parameter bounds and the probability guarantee for every deterministic algorithm.

Finally, take the expectation over $A\sim\cA$. Since~$\hat\cD$ has finite support, the two expectations can be exchanged, which gives
\begin{equation*}
  \E_{\hat I\sim\hat\cD}
  \left[\Pr_{A\sim\cA}\bigl[F_A(\hat I)\ge \rho\,\OPT(\hat I)+\gamma\bigr]\right]
  \ge1-2^{-\rho}\,.
\end{equation*}
Hence some instance in the support of~$\hat\cD$ satisfies the claim.
\end{proof}

\section{Concluding remarks}\label{sec:conclusion}

We have shown that randomization does not allow a constant competitive ratio for online weighted flow time on a single machine. Our lower bound is $\Omega(\min\{\log\log W,\log\log\log\min\{P,D\}\})$. Its dependence on~$W$ and~$P$ is exponentially weaker than in the deterministic lower bound of Bansal and Chan~\cite{BansalC09}. The best known upper bound is $O(\log\min\{P,D,W\})$, achieved by a deterministic algorithm~\cite{AzarT18}. The weak parameter dependence stems from the amplification step. Each round retains an additive increase of $\eta/2$, where $\eta=2^{-(2M+1)}$ is exponentially small in the cap~$M$. Our construction therefore uses $2^{\Theta(\rho)}$ rounds to reach the gap~$2\rho$. An amplification step whose improvement~$\eta$ is only polynomially small in the cap would lead to lower bounds that are polynomial in $\log W$ and $\log\log P$. More generally, it remains open whether randomized algorithms can beat the deterministic lower bound, for example by achieving a competitive ratio of $O(\log\log W)$.

In terms of the number~$n$ of jobs, our lower bound $\Omega(\log\log\log n)$ is weaker still, because flooding dominates the number of jobs. Each round multiplies the number of jobs by at most $m(k+1)$, so the instances in the support of~$\cD_N$ have at most $(m(k+1))^N=2^{2^{O(\rho)}}$ jobs. The stream must be long enough to make the cost before the terminal time negligible, and it consists of more than~$P$ jobs. A conversion of gaps at the terminal time into gaps in flow time that adds only polynomially many jobs would therefore give $\Omega(\log\log n)$. Whether a single machine admits an $O(\log n)$-competitive algorithm remains open. On two or more identical machines, the lower bound of Chekuri, Khanna, and Zhu~\cite{ChekuriKZ01} rules out a polylogarithmic dependence on~$n$.
