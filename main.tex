\title{Weighted Flow Time Does Not Admit\\ $O(1)$-Competitive Randomized Algorithms}

\author{%

\date{}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We study online preemptive scheduling of jobs with release times, sizes, and weights on a single machine, with the objective of minimizing total weighted flow time. Bansal and Chan (SODA~2009) showed that no deterministic online algorithm is $O(1)$-competitive for this problem. We show that no randomized online algorithm is $O(1)$-competitive against an oblivious adversary. Quantitatively, every randomized algorithm has competitive ratio $\Omega(\min\{\log\log W,\log\log\log P\})$, where $W$ and $P$ are the ratios between the largest and the smallest weight and size, respectively. We also obtain a high-probability lower bound with the same asymptotic dependence on the instance parameters.

Following Yao's principle, we construct, for each target ratio, a finite distribution over instances that defeats every deterministic algorithm. The construction compares the algorithm's unfinished weight at a random time not revealed to it with the minimum unfinished weight attainable at that time. Starting from a single job, it alternates two operations. Amplification appends a heavy job and selects a comparison checkpoint using a truncated geometric distribution, forcing the algorithm to leave either the heavy job or its earlier backlog unfinished. Averaging concatenates independent copies at decreasing time scales. A second-moment bound controls the loss from capping the averaged backlog, so we can amplify again.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

We consider the online problem of minimizing total weighted flow time on a single machine. Each job~$j$ has a release time~$r_j$, a size~$p_j$, and a weight~$w_j$, which become known at time~$r_j$. The machine processes at most one job at a time, and a job may be preempted and resumed later. If job~$j$ completes at time~$C_j$, its flow time $C_j-r_j$ is the time that it spends in the system, and the objective is to minimize $\sum_j w_j(C_j-r_j)$. An online algorithm decides at every time which job to process, without knowledge of future jobs. It is \emph{$\rho$-competitive} if, on every instance, its (expected) cost is at most $\rho$ times the optimal cost, up to an additive constant.

Without weights, always processing the job with the shortest remaining processing time is optimal, even online~\cite{Schrage68}. With weights, the problem is considerably harder. Already the offline problem is strongly NP-hard~\cite{LabetoulleLLR84}. A polynomial-time approximation scheme~\cite{ArmbrusterRW23} followed a long line of work~\cite{ChekuriK02,BansalP10,BatraGK18,FeigeKL19,RohwedderW21}.

In the online setting, the first algorithm with a nontrivial competitive ratio is due to Chekuri, Khanna, and Zhu~\cite{ChekuriKZ01}. It is $O(\log^2P)$-competitive, where $P$ is the ratio between the largest and the smallest job size, and it needs to know~$P$ in advance. Bansal and Dhamdhere~\cite{BansalD07} gave an $O(\log W)$-competitive algorithm, where $W$ is the ratio between the largest and the smallest weight. Azar and Touitou~\cite{AzarT18} gave an $O(\log P)$-competitive and an $O(\log D)$-competitive algorithm, where $D$ is the ratio between the largest and the smallest density $w_j/p_j$. They combined these results with the algorithm of Bansal and Dhamdhere into an $O(\log\min\{P,D,W\})$-competitive algorithm. A constant competitive ratio is possible with a slightly faster machine. On a machine that is faster by a factor of $1+\eps$, the Highest Density First rule is $O(1/\eps)$-competitive~\cite{BecchettiLMP06}.

It was widely believed that weighted flow time admits an $O(1)$-competitive online algorithm, until Bansal and Chan~\cite{BansalC09} showed that every deterministic online algorithm has competitive ratio
\[
  \Omega\biggl(\min\biggl\{\sqrt{\frac{\log W}{\log\log W}},\,\sqrt{\frac{\log\log P}{\log\log\log P}}\biggr\}\biggr).
\]
Their proof starts from the trivial lower bound of one and improves it step by step. Their adversary is adaptive and chooses the next jobs depending on the decisions that the algorithm has made so far. Against a randomized algorithm, however, an oblivious adversary has to fix the instance in advance, without knowing the algorithm's random choices. The adaptive construction therefore does not apply.

Randomization can be powerful when job sizes remain unknown until completion. In this model, every deterministic algorithm for unweighted flow time on a single machine has competitive ratio $\Omega(n^{1/3})$, where $n$ is the number of jobs~\cite{MotwaniPT94}. A randomized version of the multilevel feedback algorithm~\cite{KalyanasundaramP03} is $O(\log n)$-competitive~\cite{BecchettiL04}, which is best possible~\cite{MotwaniPT94}.

For weighted flow time, however, Chekuri, Khanna, and Zhu~\cite{ChekuriKZ01} showed that no randomized online algorithm is $O(1)$-competitive on $m\ge2$ identical machines, by a lower bound of $\Omega(\min\{\sqrt P,\sqrt W,(n/m)^{1/4}\})$. On a single machine, the only lower bound for randomized algorithms that we are aware of is the constant~$4/3$, also due to Chekuri, Khanna, and Zhu. Thus it has remained open whether a randomized online algorithm can be $O(1)$-competitive for weighted flow time on a single machine.

\paragraph{Our results.}
We show that this is not the case.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
No randomized online algorithm for minimizing the total weighted flow time on a single machine is $O(1)$-competitive against an oblivious adversary. More precisely, for all $\rho\ge1$ and $\gamma\ge0$, there is a probability distribution~$\cD$ over finitely many instances such that every deterministic online algorithm~$A$ satisfies
\[
  \E_{I\sim\cD}\bigl[F_A(I)\bigr]\ge \rho\cdot\E_{I\sim\cD}\bigl[\OPT(I)\bigr]+\gamma.
\]
\end{theorem}

Here $F_A(I)$ denotes the weighted flow time of the schedule that $A$ produces on the instance~$I$, and $\OPT(I)$ is the optimal weighted flow time of~$I$. By Yao's principle, the second statement implies the first, and the term~$\gamma$ rules out additive constants. The lower bound holds even when the algorithm learns each job's size and weight at its release, may process several jobs simultaneously at fractional rates, and knows the distribution~$\cD$ in advance. In particular, it may know upper bounds on~$W$ and~$P$, as well as the number of jobs, which is the same for all instances in the support of~$\cD$.

Thus, even with randomization, the competitive ratio has to grow with the parameters of the instance. Our construction gives an explicit, if weak, dependence on~$W$, $P$, and the number~$n$ of jobs.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:quant}
For all $W,P,n\ge16$, every randomized online algorithm for weighted flow time has competitive ratio
\[
  \Omega\bigl(\min\{\log\log W,\,\log\log\log P,\,\log\log\log n\}\bigr)
\]
against an oblivious adversary on instances with at most~$n$ jobs in which the ratios between the largest and the smallest weight and size are at most~$W$ and~$P$, respectively.
\end{theorem}

In particular, for $W=P=n$, the competitive ratio on instances with at most~$n$ jobs is $\Omega(\log\log\log n)$. The lower bound also holds with high probability. For every $\rho\ge1$, there is an instance on which the algorithm incurs at least $\rho$ times the optimal cost with probability at least $1-2^{-\rho}$, with the same asymptotic bounds on the instance parameters~(\cref{cor:probability}).

The dependence on~$W$ and~$P$ is exponentially weaker than in the deterministic lower bound of Bansal and Chan. To reach the lower bound~$\rho$, each amplification improves the gap only by a factor of $1+2^{-\Theta(\rho)}$. We discuss this dependence, together with the dependence on~$n$, in~\cref{sec:conclusion}.

\subsection{Technical overview}\label{sec:overview}

By Yao's principle, it suffices to construct, for every~$\rho$, a distribution for which every deterministic algorithm has expected cost at least $\rho$ times the expected optimal cost. The instance cannot react to the algorithm, so the adversary cannot choose later jobs based on which earlier jobs remain unfinished. Instead, our distributions hide the time at which the algorithm's unfinished weight is compared with the minimum possible unfinished weight.

\paragraph{Unfinished weight at a hidden time.}
Weighted flow time is the integral of the \emph{unfinished weight}, the total weight of released but uncompleted jobs. Controlling this weight at every time therefore suffices for competitiveness. Conversely, Becchetti et al.~\cite{BecchettiLMP06} showed that every $c$-competitive deterministic online algorithm is also $c$-competitive for minimizing unfinished weight at an unknown deadline. We follow this converse direction by creating a large expected gap in unfinished weight at a hidden time. A stream of small jobs then makes the gap persist long enough to dominate the weighted flow time.

\paragraph{Hard terminal distributions.}
Our building blocks are \emph{terminal distributions} over pairs $(I,T)$, where~$T$ is after all release times of~$I$. The algorithm sees the jobs of~$I$ as they arrive, but is not given~$T$. Offline, $U^*(I,T)$ is the minimum unfinished weight at~$T$. Online, we count only jobs with remaining size at least~$1$. These jobs form the \emph{robust backlog} and cannot finish before~$T+1$. We cap their total weight at~$M$. A terminal distribution is \emph{$(c,M)$-hard} if $\E[U^*(I,T)]\le1$, whereas every deterministic algorithm has expected capped robust backlog at least~$c$ (\cref{def:hard}). We call~$c$ the \emph{gap} and~$M$ the \emph{cap}. Amplification below explains why the cap is needed.

\paragraph{Flooding.}
A $(2\rho,M)$-hard terminal distribution yields the lower bound~$\rho$ by \emph{flooding} (\cref{lem:flooding}). Starting at~$T$, a long stream of jobs of weight~$1/2$ and density~$M$ keeps the machine busy. Processing old jobs creates a backlog of stream jobs. The stream is chosen so that the algorithm's unfinished weight never drops below its capped robust backlog at~$T$. Offline can instead leave weight $U^*(I,T)$ unfinished and process each stream job immediately, keeping unfinished weight at $U^*(I,T)+1/2$ (\cref{fig:flooding}). A sufficiently long stream makes this gap dominate the costs before and after it.

It thus suffices to construct $(c,M)$-hard terminal distributions with arbitrarily large gap~$c$. We start from the trivial $(1,M)$-hard distribution that consists of a single job of size~$2$ and weight~$1$ with terminal time~$1$, and we alternate two operations, amplification and averaging. Both build on the following way of combining copies of a hard distribution.

\paragraph{Copies at decreasing time scales.}
We concatenate independent samples of a hard distribution, starting copy~$i$ at the terminal time of copy~$i-1$ and stretching its times and sizes by~$s_i$. Measured at this scale, its robust backlog consists of jobs with remaining size at least~$s_i$. All later copies together have duration and total size less than~$s_i/2$ (\cref{fig:amplification}), so this backlog survives them. Given the earlier copies, copy~$i$ remains a fresh sample, and later copies arrive only from its terminal time on. Thus its capped robust backlog~$X_i$ has conditional expectation at least~$c$, even though the algorithm adapts to the past (\cref{fig:copies}). Offline can run each copy's schedule in its own window, leaving expected weight at most~$1$ per copy (\cref{lem:copies}).

\paragraph{Amplification.}
Amplification takes $k=2M+1$ copies of a $(c,M)$-hard distribution and, right after them, releases a heavy job~$J$ of weight~$2M$ whose size~$V$ is at least the total size of all copies. The terminal time is one of $k$ checkpoints $h_k<h_{k-1}<\dots<h_1$ after the release of~$J$, where checkpoint~$h_i$ leaves enough time to complete~$J$ and copies $i+1,\dots,k$. We choose the checkpoint~$h_K$ at random, with $\Pr[K\ge i]=2^{1-i}$ for $i=1,\dots,k$. For the terminal time~$h_i$, an offline schedule can complete~$J$ and copies $i+1,\dots,k$, which leaves expected weight at most~$i$ unfinished. For the earliest checkpoint~$h_k$, it can instead complete all copies and leave only~$J$, of weight $k-1$, unfinished. Hence the minimum unfinished weight at the terminal time is at most $\E[K]-\Pr[K=k]$ in expectation.

At checkpoint~$h_i$, either~$J$ still has remaining size at least~$1$, or every job in copy~$i$'s robust backlog has remaining size at least~$1$. In the latter case, that backlog contributes at~$h_i$ and all earlier checkpoints. If~$J$ is still robust, its weight compensates for the possible missing contribution from copy~$i$. Its weight is twice the cap of a copy, and the geometric probabilities make this enough. Charging each copy separately gives expected robust backlog at least $c\,\E[K]$ (\cref{fig:amp-accounting}). Offline saves $\Pr[K=k]$ relative to~$\E[K]$, so normalizing the weights improves the gap by at least a factor of $1+\eta$, where $\eta=2^{-k}$~(\cref{lem:amplification}).

\paragraph{Averaging.}
Amplification improves the gap but increases the cap, making the next improvement exponentially smaller. Averaging brings the cap back down. Given a $(c,M)$-hard distribution, it concatenates~$m$ copies and divides all weights by~$m$. Their capped robust backlogs $X_i\in[0,M]$ need not be independent, but have conditional expectations at least~$c$. A second-moment bound shows that their average, capped at~$c$, has expectation at least $c-M/\sqrt m$ (\cref{lem:averaging}). Thus we can reduce the cap to~$c$ while losing arbitrarily little from the gap.

\paragraph{Putting things together.}
We fix the cap $M=\lceil4\rho\rceil$ and alternate amplification and averaging. Amplification increases the gap by at least~$\eta$, and averaging loses at most~$\eta/2$ while returning the cap to at most~$M$. Each round therefore adds at least~$\eta/2$ to the gap. After $2^{O(\rho)}$ rounds it reaches~$2\rho$~(\cref{thm:hard}). Each round combines $2^{O(\rho)}$ copies, giving the bounds on~$W$, $P$, and~$n$ in~\cref{thm:quant}.

\paragraph{Comparison with the deterministic lower bound.}
Our construction builds on the deterministic lower bound of Bansal and Chan~\cite{BansalC09}, who introduced the idea of improving a lower bound step by step. Several of our ingredients appear there in adaptive form. They measure unfinished weight using jobs with large remaining size, run subinstances at decreasing scales, release a final heavy job, and convert unfinished weight into flow time with a stream of small jobs. Their adversary observes the algorithm. Each subinstance ends when the algorithm has fallen behind, and the continuation after the heavy job depends on how the algorithm processes it. Our hidden random terminal time makes amplification oblivious. The conditional analysis of copies (\cref{lem:copies}) handles algorithms that adapt to earlier copies, while the cap and averaging make amplification repeatable although its guarantee holds only in expectation.

\subsection{Further related work}

Without weights, the Shortest Remaining Processing Time rule is $O(\log\min\{P,n/m\})$-competitive on $m$ identical machines. This is best possible for $m\ge2$, even for randomized algorithms~\cite{LeonardiR07}. Related models include weighted flow time with speed augmentation~\cite{BecchettiLMP06,AnandGK12} and flow time with uncertain job sizes~\cite{AzarLT21,AzarLT22}.

\Cref{sec:prelim} introduces the model and Yao's principle. \Cref{sec:hard} defines hard terminal distributions and shows that they imply \cref{thm:main}. \Cref{sec:construction} constructs hard terminal distributions, \cref{sec:quantitative} bounds the instance parameters and proves the high-probability corollary, and~\cref{sec:conclusion} discusses open problems.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec:prelim}

\paragraph{Instances and schedules.}
An \emph{instance}~$I$ is a finite set of jobs. Each job $j\in I$ has a release time $r_j\ge0$, a size $p_j>0$, and a weight $w_j>0$. We identify a job with the triple $(r_j,p_j,w_j)$, so an algorithm knows nothing about a job but these parameters. For a set~$E$ of jobs, we write $w(E)=\sum_{j\in E}w_j$ and $p(E)=\sum_{j\in E}p_j$. For a positive integer~$n$, we write $[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}$.

A \emph{schedule} of~$I$ assigns to each job $j\in I$ a measurable rate function $x_j\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,1]$ such that $x_j(t)=0$ for $t<r_j$, $\sum_{j\in I}x_j(t)\le1$ for all~$t$, and $\int_0^\infty x_j(t)\dd t\le p_j$. The \emph{remaining size} of~$j$ at time~$t$ is $p_j-\int_0^t x_j(t')\dd t'$, a continuous and non-increasing function of~$t$. Job~$j$ \emph{completes} at the first time~$C_j$ at which its remaining size is zero ($C_j=\infty$ if there is no such time), and it is \emph{unfinished} at time~$t$ if $r_j\le t<C_j$. The weighted flow time of the schedule is
\[
  \sum_{j\in I}w_j(C_j-r_j)=\int_0^\infty U(t)\dd t,
\]
where the \emph{unfinished weight} $U(t)$ is the total weight of the jobs that are unfinished at time~$t$. We denote by $\OPT(I)$ the infimum of the weighted flow time over all schedules of~$I$.

Schedules with rates in $\{0,1\}$ are the usual preemptive schedules. Allowing fractional rates does not change the optimum. Given a fractional schedule, the preemptive Earliest Deadline First rule, with that schedule's completion times as deadlines, completes every job no later than in the given schedule. For online algorithms, fractional rates only strengthen our lower bounds.

\paragraph{Online algorithms.}
A \emph{deterministic online algorithm}~$A$ maps every instance~$I$ to a schedule $A(I)$ of~$I$ subject to the following non-anticipation condition: for every $t\ge0$ and all instances $I$ and $I'$ that contain the same jobs with release times before~$t$, the schedules $A(I)$ and $A(I')$ assign the same rates to these jobs during $[0,t)$. In other words, the decisions of~$A$ before time~$t$ depend only on the jobs released before~$t$, including their sizes and weights. In particular, these jobs have the same remaining sizes at time~$t$ in $A(I)$ and in $A(I')$. We write $F_A(I)$ for the weighted flow time of $A(I)$. A deterministic online algorithm may be designed for a specific input distribution. For example, it may use the number of jobs if this number is the same for all instances in the support. It is nevertheless defined on all instances, because the proofs of~\cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:scaling} and~\cref{lem:copies} run it on auxiliary instances.

A \emph{randomized online algorithm}~$\cA$ is a probability distribution over deterministic online algorithms such that $F_A(I)$ is a random variable for every instance~$I$ when $A\sim\cA$. It is \emph{$\rho$-competitive} (against an oblivious adversary) if there is a constant~$\Gamma$ such that $\E_{A\sim\cA}[F_A(I)]\le \rho\cdot\OPT(I)+\Gamma$ for every instance~$I$. We use the easy direction of Yao's principle~\cite{Yao77} (see also~\cite{BorodinE98}).

\begin{lemma}[Yao's principle]\label{lem:yao}
Let $\cD$ be a probability distribution over finitely many instances, and let $\rho>0$ and $\gamma\in\RR$ be such that every deterministic online algorithm~$A$ satisfies $\E_{I\sim\cD}[F_A(I)]\ge \rho\cdot\E_{I\sim\cD}[\OPT(I)]+\gamma$. Then for every randomized online algorithm~$\cA$, there is an instance~$I$ in the support of~$\cD$ with $\E_{A\sim\cA}[F_A(I)]\ge \rho\cdot\OPT(I)+\gamma$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $\E_{A\sim\cA}[F_A(I)]=\infty$ for some~$I$ in the support of~$\cD$, this instance satisfies the claim. Otherwise, taking the expectation of the assumption over $A\sim\cA$ and using linearity of expectation over the finitely many instances in the support of~$\cD$, we obtain
\[
  \E_{I\sim\cD}\bigl[\E_{A\sim\cA}[F_A(I)]-\rho\cdot\OPT(I)\bigr]\ge\gamma.
\]
Hence some instance in the support of~$\cD$ satisfies the claim.
\end{proof}

\section{Hard terminal distributions}\label{sec:hard}

We first define hardness at a hidden terminal time and show how it yields a lower bound for weighted flow time. This reduces~\cref{thm:main} to the construction of hard terminal distributions in~\cref{sec:construction}.

A \emph{terminal instance} is a pair $(I,T)$ of an instance~$I$ and a \emph{terminal time} $T>0$ such that all jobs of~$I$ are released before~$T$. A \emph{terminal distribution} is a probability distribution over finitely many terminal instances. An online algorithm processes only~$I$, so its schedule $A(I)$ does not depend on~$T$. The algorithm may know the distribution in advance. Since~$I$ and~$T$ can be correlated, it may also learn something about~$T$ from the jobs it sees.

For a terminal instance $(I,T)$, let $U^*(I,T)$ be the minimum total weight of the jobs that a schedule of~$I$ leaves unfinished at time~$T$. For a deterministic online algorithm~$A$, the \emph{robust backlog} $R_A(I,T)$ of~$A$ at time~$T$ is the set of jobs of~$I$ whose remaining size in $A(I)$ at time~$T$ is at least~$1$. These jobs are unfinished at time~$T$, and none of them can be completed before time~$T+1$. For $M>0$, the \emph{capped robust backlog} is
\[
  \varphi_A^M(I,T)=\min\bigl\{M,\,w(R_A(I,T))\bigr\}.
\]

\begin{definition}\label{def:hard}
Let $0<c\le M$. A terminal distribution~$\cD$ is \emph{$(c,M)$-hard} if
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
	\item\label{def:hard-offline} $\E\bigl[U^*(I,T)\bigr]\le1$ and
	\item\label{def:hard-online} $\E\bigl[\varphi_A^M(I,T)\bigr]\ge c$ for every deterministic online algorithm~$A$,
\end{enumerate}
where the expectations are over $(I,T)\sim\cD$. We call~$c$ the \emph{gap} and~$M$ the \emph{cap}.
\end{definition}

The cap is needed for amplification (\cref{sec:amplification}), where a heavy job compensates for copy backlogs that may no longer be robust. Limiting each copy's contribution to~$M$ makes this compensation possible. The following observation provides the starting point of our construction and two rules that we use repeatedly.

\begin{observation}\label{obs:basic}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item\label{obs:base} The terminal distribution of  the single instance $(I,T)$ that consists of a single job with release time~$0$, size~$2$, and weight~$1$, together with the terminal time~$1$, is $(1,M)$-hard for every $M\ge1$.
\item\label{obs:monotone} If~$\cD$ is $(c,M)$-hard, then~$\cD$ is $(c',M')$-hard for all $0<c'\le c$ and $M'\ge M$.
\item\label{obs:scaling} Let $\lambda>0$ and $0<c\le M$, and let~$\cD$ be a terminal distribution such that $\E[U^*(I,T)]\le\lambda$ and $\E[\varphi_A^{\lambda M}(I,T)]\ge\lambda c$ for every deterministic online algorithm~$A$, where the expectations are over $(I,T)\sim\cD$. Then the terminal distribution obtained from~$\cD$ by dividing all weights by~$\lambda$ is $(c,M)$-hard.
\end{enumerate}
\end{observation}

\begin{proof}
\ref{obs:base}~No schedule completes the job by time~$1$, and in every schedule its remaining size at time~$1$ is at least~$1$. Hence $U^*(I,T)=1$ and $\varphi_A^M(I,T)=\min\{M,1\}=1$.

\ref{obs:monotone}~\Cref{def:hard}\ref{def:hard-offline} involves neither the gap nor the cap, so it carries over from~$(c,M)$ to~$(c',M')$. For \cref{def:hard}\ref{def:hard-online}, since $M'\ge M$, every deterministic online algorithm~$A$ and every terminal instance $(I,T)$ satisfy $\varphi_A^{M'}(I,T)\ge\varphi_A^M(I,T)$. Taking the expectation over $(I,T)\sim\cD$ gives $\E[\varphi_A^{M'}(I,T)]\ge\E[\varphi_A^M(I,T)]\ge c\ge c'$.

\ref{obs:scaling}~For an instance~$I$, let $I/\lambda$ denote the instance with all weights divided by~$\lambda$. Since $U^*(I/\lambda,T)=U^*(I,T)/\lambda$, we have $\E[U^*(I/\lambda,T)]\le1$, where here and below the expectations are over $(I,T)\sim\cD$. Let~$A$ be a deterministic online algorithm. As~$A$ need not treat~$I$ and $I/\lambda$ alike, we apply the assumption to the algorithm~$A_\lambda$ that on input~$I$ runs~$A$ on $I/\lambda$ and uses the same rates, that is, $A_\lambda(I)=A(I/\lambda)$. Since $I$ and $I/\lambda$ have the same release times, $A_\lambda$ satisfies the non-anticipation condition. The robust backlogs of~$A$ on $(I/\lambda,T)$ and of~$A_\lambda$ on $(I,T)$ consist of the same jobs up to the scaling of their weights, so $\varphi_A^M(I/\lambda,T)=\varphi_{A_\lambda}^{\lambda M}(I,T)/\lambda$. By assumption, the right-hand side has expectation at least~$c$.
\end{proof}

\begin{samepage}
\begin{lemma}[Flooding]\label{lem:flooding}
Let $(I,T)$ be a terminal instance, let $M>0$, and let $L$ be a positive multiple of $d=1/(2M)$. Form~$\hat I$ by adding jobs of size~$d$ and weight~$1/2$ at times $T,T+d,\dots,T+L-d$. Then every deterministic online algorithm~$A$ satisfies
\begin{align*}
  F_A(\hat I)&\ge L\,\varphi_A^M(I,T)\,,\\
  \OPT(\hat I)&\le L\bigl(U^*(I,T)+\tfrac12\bigr)+w(I)\bigl(T+p(I)\bigr)\,.
\end{align*}
\end{lemma}
\end{samepage}

\begin{proof}
Call the jobs of~$I$ \emph{old} and the added jobs \emph{stream jobs}.

\emph{Online cost.} Fix~$A$. Since the stream starts at~$T$, non-anticipation implies that the robust backlog at~$T$ is the same in $A(I)$ and $A(\hat I)$. Write $\varphi=\varphi_A^M(I,T)$. For $0\le\theta<L$, let~$y$ be the processing that the old jobs receive during $[T,T+\theta)$. If $y<1$, every job in the robust backlog is still unfinished, so the unfinished weight is at least~$\varphi$. If $y\ge1$, the stream jobs released by time~$T+\theta$ have total size $d(\lfloor\theta/d\rfloor+1)>\theta$ and receive at most $\theta-y$ processing. Their total remaining size therefore exceeds~$y$. More than $y/d$ stream jobs are unfinished, with total weight greater than $y/(2d)=My\ge M\ge\varphi$. Thus the unfinished weight stays at least~$\varphi$ throughout the stream (\cref{fig:flooding}). Integration gives $F_A(\hat I)\ge L\varphi$.

\emph{Optimal cost.} Until~$T$, follow a schedule that leaves weight $U^*(I,T)$ unfinished. Process every stream job immediately after its release, then finish the remaining old jobs. The old jobs contribute at most $w(I)T$ before~$T$, exactly $L\,U^*(I,T)$ during the stream, and at most $w(I)p(I)$ afterwards. The stream jobs contribute $L/2$. Adding these terms proves the bound.
\end{proof}

The main technical result is the existence of hard terminal distributions with arbitrarily large gaps.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:hard}
For every $\rho\ge1$, there is a $(2\rho,M)$-hard terminal distribution with $M=\lceil4\rho\rceil$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:main}]
Let $\rho\ge1$ and $\gamma\ge0$, and take a $(2\rho,M)$-hard terminal distribution~$\cD$ from~\cref{thm:hard}. Since its support is finite, there is a constant~$B$ such that $w(I)(T+p(I))\le B$ throughout the support. Apply~\cref{lem:flooding} with the same length~$L$ to every terminal instance, where $L$ is a positive multiple of $1/(2M)$ and $L\ge2B+2\gamma/\rho$. For the resulting distribution~$\hat\cD$, hardness and the two bounds of the lemma give
\begin{equation*}
  \E[F_A(\hat I)]-\rho\,\E[\OPT(\hat I)]
  \ge 2\rho L-\rho\bigl(\tfrac32L+B\bigr)
  =\rho(L/2-B)\ge\gamma\,,
\end{equation*}
for every deterministic online algorithm~$A$. This proves the distributional statement. For a randomized algorithm~$\cA$ and any additive constant~$\Gamma\ge0$, apply~\cref{lem:yao} with $\gamma=\Gamma+1$. It gives an instance~$\hat I$ with $\E_{A\sim\cA}[F_A(\hat I)]\ge \rho\,\OPT(\hat I)+\Gamma+1$. Since $\rho$ and~$\Gamma$ are arbitrary, no randomized online algorithm is $O(1)$-competitive.
\end{proof}

\section{Constructing hard terminal distributions}\label{sec:construction}

We prove~\cref{thm:hard} by alternating two operations on hard terminal distributions. Amplification (\cref{sec:amplification}) multiplies the gap by $1+\eta$ for a small $\eta>0$ that depends on the cap, but it increases the cap. Averaging (\cref{sec:averaging}) reduces the cap to the input gap while losing only a little from that gap. Both operations concatenate independent copies at decreasing time scales, which we analyze first.

\subsection{Copying a terminal distribution}\label{sec:copies}

We define the concatenation of copies and show that an offline schedule can handle the copies separately, whereas an online algorithm faces each copy as a fresh sample of the distribution.

Let~$\cD$ be a terminal distribution. A \emph{bound} for~$\cD$ is a number $H\ge1$ such that $T\le H$ and $p(I)\le H$ for every terminal instance $(I,T)$ in its support. Fix a bound $H$ for $\cD$ and a positive integer~$m$. 
We will construct $m$ terminal instances, which we call \emph{copies}, based on $m$ independent samples $(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_m,T_m)$ of $\cD$ by stretching the $i$th sample in time and size by a factor~$s_i$, which decreases with~$i$. 
To define these factors, we first define quantities $S_0,\dots,S_m$ such that each~$S_i$ will bound both the duration and the total size of copies $i+1,\dots,m$. Set $S_m=0$ and, for $i=m,\dots,1$, define $s_i=2S_i+2$ and $S_{i-1}=S_i+Hs_i$.
Let $V=S_0$. By construction, for every $i\in[m]$,
it holds that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scales}
  S_i=H\sum_{j=i+1}^m s_j
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  s_i-S_i=S_i+2\,,
\end{equation}
where the first identity also holds for~$i=0$. Set $u_0=0$ and $u_i=u_{i-1}+s_iT_i$. For each job $j\in I_i$, \emph{copy~$i$} contains a job with release time $u_{i-1}+s_ir_j$, size $s_ip_j$, and weight~$w_j$. Thus copy~$i$ is~$I_i$ stretched in time and size by~$s_i$, started at~$u_{i-1}$, and ending at its terminal time~$u_i$. The \emph{concatenation}~$\bar I$ is the union of the copies, and $\tau=u_m$ is its \emph{end}. 
The scales ensure that copies $i+1,\dots,m$ are released during the time interval $[u_i,\tau)$ of length at most~$S_i$. For a deterministic online algorithm~$A$, the robust backlog of~$A$ in copy~$i$, measured at scale~$s_i$, consists of the jobs of copy~$i$ whose remaining size in~$A(\bar I)$ at the terminal time~$u_i$ is at least~$s_i$. As a job receives at most~$S_i$ processing during $[u_i,\tau)$, these jobs survive the later copies: they are still unfinished at~$\tau$, with remaining size at least $s_i-S_i=S_i+2$ (\cref{fig:copies}).

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:copies}
Let~$\cD$ be a $(c,M)$-hard terminal distribution, and let~$\bar I$ be the concatenation of $m$ copies of~$\cD$ with end~$\tau$, as above.
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item\label{it:geometry} The jobs of copy~$i$ are released during $[u_{i-1},u_i)$. For every $i\in\{0,\dots,m\}$, copies $i+1,\dots,m$ have total size at most~$S_i$, and $\tau-u_i\le S_i$. In particular, $p(\bar I)\le V$ and $\tau\le V$.
\item\label{it:offline} There is a schedule of~$\bar I$ in which, for every $i\in[m]$, the jobs of copy~$i$ that are unfinished at time~$\tau$ have total weight $B_i=U^*(I_i,T_i)$. Moreover, $\E[B_i]\le1$.
\item\label{it:online} Let~$A$ be a deterministic online algorithm. For $i\in[m]$, let~$E_i$ be the set of jobs of copy~$i$ whose remaining size at time~$u_i$ in $A(\bar I)$ is at least~$s_i$, and let $X_i=\min\{M,w(E_i)\}$. Then $X_i$ is determined by $(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_i,T_i)$, and $\E\bigl[X_i\bigm|(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_{i-1},T_{i-1})\bigr]\ge c$.
Moreover, every job of~$E_i$ has remaining size at least $S_i+2$ at time~$\tau$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

In part~\ref{it:online}, the set~$E_i$ is the robust backlog measured in the units of copy~$i$. After shrinking time and sizes by~$s_i$, its jobs have remaining size at least~$1$ at that copy's terminal time.

\begin{proof}
\ref{it:geometry}~A job of copy~$i$ is released at time $u_{i-1}+s_ir_j$ with $0\le r_j<T_i$, hence during $[u_{i-1},u_i)$. Copy~$i'$ has total size $s_{i'}\,p(I_{i'})\le s_{i'}H$, and it is released during the interval $[u_{i'-1},u_{i'})$ of length $s_{i'}T_{i'}\le s_{i'}H$. Summing over $i'>i$ and using~\eqref{eq:scales} proves the bounds on the later copies. Taking $i=0$ gives $p(\bar I)\le V$ and $\tau\le V$.

\ref{it:offline}~During $[u_{i-1},u_i)$, run a schedule of~$I_i$ that leaves weight $B_i=U^*(I_i,T_i)$ unfinished at~$T_i$, stretched in time and size by~$s_i$. Do not process copy~$i$ afterwards. Stretching preserves feasibility, and the time windows are disjoint, so these schedules combine into a schedule of~$\bar I$ that leaves weight~$B_i$ from each copy unfinished at~$\tau$. Since $(I_i,T_i)$ is a sample from the $(c,M)$-hard distribution~$\cD$, we have $\E[B_i]=\E[U^*(I_i,T_i)]\le1$ by~\cref{def:hard}\ref{def:hard-offline}.

\ref{it:online}~Fix $i\in[m]$ and condition on any realization of $(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_{i-1},T_{i-1})$ of positive probability. The preceding copies and~$u_{i-1}$ are now fixed. Define an algorithm~$A_i$ on an arbitrary input~$I$ by simulating~$A$ on those preceding copies followed by~$I$, stretched by~$s_i$ and started at~$u_{i-1}$. At time~$t$, algorithm~$A_i$ gives a job the rate that~$A$ gives its copy at time~$u_{i-1}+s_it$. Processing that~$A$ assigns to preceding jobs becomes idle time for~$A_i$. These rates define a feasible online schedule, and remaining sizes are divided by~$s_i$.

On input~$I_i$, the simulated instance and~$\bar I$ contain the same jobs released before~$u_i$, because all later copies arrive at~$u_i$ or later. Non-anticipation therefore gives identical remaining sizes at~$u_i$. In particular, $X_i$ is determined by the first~$i$ samples and we have that $X_i=\varphi_{A_i}^M(I_i,T_i)$.
To bound the conditional expectation of~$X_i$, we apply~\cref{def:hard}\ref{def:hard-online} to~$A_i$. Here~$A_i$ is a deterministic online algorithm: if two inputs contain the same jobs with release times before~$t$, the simulated instances contain the same jobs with release times before $u_{i-1}+s_it$, so~$A_i$ inherits non-anticipation from~$A$. Moreover, $A_i$ depends only on the fixed preceding samples, not on~$(I_i,T_i)$. Since the samples are independent, $(I_i,T_i)$ is a fresh sample from~$\cD$: given this realization, its distribution is still~$\cD$. As~$\cD$ is $(c,M)$-hard, we obtain
\begin{equation*}
  \E\bigl[X_i\bigm|(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_{i-1},T_{i-1})\bigr]
  =\E_{(I,T)\sim\cD}\bigl[\varphi_{A_i}^M(I,T)\bigr]
  \ge c\,.
\end{equation*}

Finally, a job of~$E_i$ has remaining size at least~$s_i$ at~$u_i$ and receives at most $\tau-u_i\le S_i$ processing before~$\tau$. Its remaining size at~$\tau$ is therefore at least $s_i-S_i=S_i+2$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Amplification}\label{sec:amplification}

Amplification appends a heavy job to $k$ copies of a terminal distribution and hides the terminal time among $k$ checkpoints after the release of this job. Let $M\ge1$ be an integer and $k=2M+1$. Let~$\cD$ be a terminal distribution with a bound~$H$, and let~$\bar I$ be the concatenation of~$k$ copies of~$\cD$, with the notation of \cref{sec:copies} for $m=k$. We add a job~$J$ with release time~$\tau$, size~$V$, and weight~$2M$, and we define for each $i\in [k]$ the \emph{checkpoint} $h_i=\tau+V+S_i$.
Since $S_1>S_2>\dots>S_k=0$, we have $h_1>h_2>\dots>h_k=\tau+V$. Independently of the copies, we draw an integer $K\in[k]$ with $\Pr[K\ge i]=2^{1-i}$ for all $i\in[k]$. Equivalently, $K$ counts fair coin flips up to and including the first head, truncated at~$k$. We have $\E[K]=\sum_{i=1}^k\Pr[K\ge i]=2-2^{1-k}$ and $\Pr[K=k]=2^{1-k}$. Set
\[
  \beta:=\E[K]-\Pr[K=k]=2-2^{2-k}.
\]
The \emph{amplified} terminal distribution $\Amp_M(\cD)$ is the distribution of the terminal instance $(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)$ with all weights divided by~$\beta$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:amplification}
Let $M\ge1$ be an integer, $k=2M+1$, and $\eta=2^{-k}$. If~$\cD$ is $(c,M)$-hard, then $\Amp_M(\cD)$ is $((1+\eta)c,(k+1)M)$-hard. Moreover, every terminal instance $(I,T)$ in its support satisfies $U^*(I,T)\le2M$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
All jobs of $\bar I\cup\{J\}$ are released by time $\tau<h_K$, and the support is finite, so $\Amp_M(\cD)$ is a terminal distribution. We show that the terminal instance $(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)$, before the weights are divided by~$\beta$, satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:amp-goal}
  \E\bigl[U^*(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)\bigr]\le\beta
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \E\bigl[\varphi_A^{(k+1)M}(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)\bigr]\ge c\,\E[K]
\end{equation}
for every deterministic online algorithm~$A$, where the expectations are over the copies and~$K$. To apply~\cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:scaling}, first increase the cap to $\beta(k+1)M$, which preserves the online bound because $\beta\ge1$. Dividing all weights by~$\beta$ gives the claimed cap, and $\beta\le2$ yields the gain
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{\E[K]}{\beta}=1+\frac{2^{1-k}}{\beta}\ge1+2^{-k}=1+\eta\,.
\end{equation*}

\emph{Offline.} Follow the schedule of~\cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:offline} until~$\tau$. At every checkpoint~$h_i$, two continuations are feasible. We can complete~$J$ and the remaining jobs of copies $i+1,\dots,k$ in the available $V+S_i$ time units, or complete all remaining old jobs in~$V$ time units, by~\cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:geometry}. Thus
\begin{equation*}
  U^*(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_i)\le\min\left\{\sum_{j\le i}B_j,\,2M\right\}\,.
\end{equation*}
Since~$K$ is independent of the copies and $\E[B_j]\le1$, conditioning on $K=i$ bounds the expected unfinished weight by $\min\{i,2M\}$. Hence, using $2M=k-1$,
\begin{equation*}
  \E[U^*(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)]
  \le\E[\min\{K,k-1\}]
  =\E[K]-\Pr[K=k]=\beta\,.
\end{equation*}
The same pointwise bound, after normalization, gives unfinished weight at most $2M/\beta\le2M$.

\emph{Online.} Fix a deterministic online algorithm~$A$ and the copies $(I_1,T_1),\dots,(I_k,T_k)$. The variable~$K$ affects only the terminal time, so the schedule $A(\bar I\cup\{J\})$ is fixed. Let~$E_j$ and~$X_j$ be as in~\cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:online}. Adding~$J$ at~$\tau$ leaves all remaining sizes up to~$\tau$ unchanged by non-anticipation, so the lemma applies to this schedule as well.

If~$J$ is not robust at~$h_j$, then it received more than $V-1$ processing during $[\tau,h_j)$, leaving less than $S_j+1$ processing for the old jobs. Every job of~$E_j$ had remaining size at least $S_j+2$ at~$\tau$, so it is still robust at~$h_j$, and hence at every earlier checkpoint~$h_i$ with $i\ge j$.

For each copy~$j$, assign a charge as follows. If~$J$ is not robust at~$h_j$, charge~$X_j$ at every checkpoint~$h_i$ with $i\ge j$. Otherwise, charge~$2M$ only at~$h_j$. The first charge is supported by~$E_j$, and the second by~$J$. At any checkpoint~$h_i$, copy charges use disjoint sets of robust jobs, and~$J$ is charged at most once. If~$J$ is charged, $E_i$ contributes no charge, so the total charge is at most $(k-1)M+2M=(k+1)M$. Without a charge to~$J$, the total is at most~$kM$. Thus the total charge is bounded by the capped robust backlog (\cref{fig:amp-accounting}).

The expected charge for copy~$j$ is either $X_j\Pr[K\ge j]$ or $2M\Pr[K=j]$. Both are at least $X_j\Pr[K\ge j]$, because $X_j\le M$ and $2\Pr[K=j]\ge\Pr[K\ge j]$. Summing over the copies gives
\begin{equation*}
  \E_K\bigl[\varphi_A^{(k+1)M}(\bar I\cup\{J\},h_K)\bigr]
  \ge\sum_{j=1}^k\Pr[K\ge j]X_j\,.
\end{equation*}
Taking expectation over the copies and using $\E[X_j]\ge c$ from~\cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:online} yields $c\,\E[K]$, as required in~\eqref{eq:amp-goal}.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:amplification}
Apply amplification with $M=1$ to the $(1,1)$-hard single-job distribution of~\cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:base}. Then $k=3$, and the checkpoint probabilities are $(1/2,1/4,1/4)$. The offline schedules leave weights at most $1$, $2$, and~$2$, giving expected weight at most $3/2=\beta$. The charging argument gives expected capped robust backlog at least $\E[K]=7/4$. Normalization therefore yields gap at least $7/6$. The gain comes from offline saving one unit at checkpoint~$h_3$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Averaging}\label{sec:averaging}

Averaging reduces the cap to the input gap. For a terminal distribution~$\cD$ with a bound~$H$ and a positive integer~$m$, the \emph{averaged} terminal distribution $\Avg_m(\cD)$ is the distribution of the terminal instance $(\bar I,\tau)$, where~$\bar I$ is the concatenation of~$m$ copies of~$\cD$ and~$\tau$ is its end, with all weights divided by~$m$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:averaging}
Let~$\cD$ be a $(c,M)$-hard terminal distribution and let~$m$ be a positive integer. For $(I,T)\sim\Avg_m(\cD)$,
\begin{equation*}
  \E[U^*(I,T)]\le1
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \E[\varphi_A^c(I,T)]\ge c-\frac{M}{\sqrt m}
\end{equation*}
for every deterministic online algorithm~$A$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
All jobs of~$\bar I$ are released before~$\tau$ by~\cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:geometry}. We first work before dividing weights by~$m$. The weight-scaling argument in the proof of~\cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:scaling} then divides both bounds by~$m$. The offline bound is $\E[U^*(\bar I,\tau)]\le m$, since~\cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:offline} gives $U^*(\bar I,\tau)\le B_1+\dots+B_m$.

Fix~$A$, and let~$E_i$ and~$X_i$ be as in~\cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:online}. Track the shortfall after~$i$ copies by
\begin{equation*}
  D_i=\left(ic-\sum_{j=1}^iX_j\right)^+,\qquad D_0=0\,.
\end{equation*}
Every job of~$E_i$ has remaining size at least $S_i+2\ge2$ at~$\tau$. The robust backlog therefore contains the pairwise disjoint sets $E_1,\dots,E_m$, giving
\begin{equation*}
  \varphi_A^{cm}(\bar I,\tau)\ge\min\left\{cm,\sum_{i=1}^mX_i\right\}=cm-D_m\,.
\end{equation*}

A previous surplus can only reduce the next shortfall, so $D_i\le(D_{i-1}+c-X_i)^+$. Squaring and using $0\le X_i\le M$ and $c\le M$, we obtain
\begin{equation*}
  D_i^2\le(D_{i-1}+c-X_i)^2
  \le D_{i-1}^2+2D_{i-1}(c-X_i)+M^2\,.
\end{equation*}
Given the first $i-1$ samples, $D_{i-1}$ is fixed and nonnegative, and~$X_i$ has conditional expectation at least~$c$ by~\cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:online}. Thus the middle term has nonpositive expectation, giving $\E[D_i^2]\le\E[D_{i-1}^2]+M^2$. Induction yields $\E[D_m^2]\le mM^2$, and Cauchy--Schwarz gives
\begin{equation*}
  \E[\varphi_A^{cm}(\bar I,\tau)]
  \ge cm-\E[D_m]
  \ge cm-\sqrt{\E[D_m^2]}
  \ge cm-M\sqrt m\,.
\end{equation*}
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of \texorpdfstring{\cref{thm:hard}}{Theorem 3.4}}\label{sec:iteration}

Fix $\rho\ge1$. We keep the cap $M=\lceil4\rho\rceil$ throughout the construction. Set
\begin{equation*}
  k=2M+1,\qquad \eta=2^{-k},\qquad
  m=\left(\frac{2(k+1)M}{\eta}\right)^2,\qquad
  N=\frac{M-2}{\eta}\,.
\end{equation*}
Both~$m$ and~$N$ are positive integers. Start with the single-job distribution~$\cD_0$ of~\cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:base}, and for $q=1,\dots,N$ set
\begin{equation*}
  \cD_q=\Avg_m(\Amp_M(\cD_{q-1})),\qquad
  c_q=1+q\eta/2\,.
\end{equation*}
Each operation may use any bound for its input distribution. We fix specific bounds in~\cref{sec:quantitative}. We call one amplification followed by one averaging a \emph{round}.

We prove inductively that~$\cD_q$ is $(c_q,M)$-hard, with $c_0=1$. The base case is~\cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:base}. In round~$q$, amplification gives gap $(1+\eta)c_{q-1}$ and cap $(k+1)M$. Averaging loses at most $(k+1)M/\sqrt m=\eta/2$ by~\cref{lem:averaging}, giving gap at least
\begin{equation*}
  (1+\eta)c_{q-1}-\eta/2
  \ge c_{q-1}+\eta/2=c_q\,,
\end{equation*}
since $c_{q-1}\ge1$. The averaged distribution has cap $(1+\eta)c_{q-1}<M$, because $c_{q-1}<c_N=M/2$ and $\eta<1$. By~\cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:monotone}, the result is $(c_q,M)$-hard. Finally, $c_N=M/2\ge2\rho$, which proves~\cref{thm:hard}.

By induction on~$q$, all instances in the support of~$\cD_q$ have the same number of jobs, and flooding (\cref{lem:flooding}) adds the same number of jobs to each instance. Hence our lower bounds hold even for algorithms that know the number of jobs in advance.

\section{Dependence on \texorpdfstring{$W$, $P$, and~$n$}{W, P, and n}}\label{sec:quantitative}

For an instance~$I$, let $W(I)=\max_{j\in I}w_j/\min_{j\in I}w_j$ and $P(I)=\max_{j\in I}p_j/\min_{j\in I}p_j$ be its weight and size ratios, and let~$|I|$ be its number of jobs. In this section, all logarithms are binary. We analyze the construction of \cref{sec:iteration} and prove the following refinement of \cref{thm:main}.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:quant-refined}
There is an absolute constant $C\ge1$ such that for all $\rho\ge1$ and $\gamma\ge0$, there is a probability distribution~$\hat\cD$ over finitely many instances such that every instance~$\hat I$ in its support satisfies
\[
  W(\hat I)\le2^{2^{C\rho}},\qquad P(\hat I)\le2^{2^{2^{C\rho}}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad|\hat I|\le2^{2^{2^{C\rho}}},
\]
and every deterministic online algorithm~$A$ satisfies $\E_{\hat I\sim\hat\cD}[F_A(\hat I)]\ge \rho\cdot\E_{\hat I\sim\hat\cD}[\OPT(\hat I)]+\gamma$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $M$, $k$, $\eta$, $m$, $N$, and $\cD_0,\dots,\cD_N$ be as in \cref{sec:iteration}. We obtain~$\hat\cD$ by flooding~$\cD_N$. We first bound the weights and sizes, then choose the stream length to obtain the cost bound and count the jobs.

It suffices to prove the claim for $\gamma=1$. Multiplying all weights by $\lambda=\max\{1,\gamma\}$ preserves $W$, $P$, and~$n$ and scales both costs by~$\lambda$. For each algorithm~$A$ on the scaled instances, apply the unit-gap guarantee to $A'(I)=A(\lambda I)$, as in the weight-scaling argument of~\cref{obs:basic}\ref{obs:scaling}.

We apply~\cref{lem:flooding} to~$\cD_N$, using a common stream length~$L$ specified below. The stream jobs have weight~$1/2$ and size $1/(2M)$.

\emph{Weights.} All weights in~$\cD_q$ lie in $[(2m)^{-q},1]$. Indeed, the initial weight is~$1$, and each round divides all old weights and the new heavy weight~$2M$ by~$\beta m\in[m,2m]$. The claim follows by induction, since $m\ge2M$. The stream weight~$1/2$ lies in the same interval for $q=N\ge1$, so $W(\hat I)\le(2m)^N$.

\emph{Sizes.} The backward recurrence for the copy scales gives $S_{i-1}+1=(2H+1)(S_i+1)$. A concatenation of~$r$ copies with bound $H\ge3$ therefore has
\begin{equation*}
  V=(2H+1)^r-1\le H^{2r}\,.
\end{equation*}
Amplification has total size at most~$2V$ and terminal time at most $\tau+V+S_1\le3V$, so $H^{3k}$ is a bound for its output. Averaging with this bound then gives a bound of $H^{6km}$. Thus, in round~$q$, use $H_{q-1}$ for amplification and $H_{q-1}^{3k}$ for averaging, where
\begin{equation*}
  H_0=3,\qquad H_q=H_{q-1}^{6km}=3^{(6km)^q}\,.
\end{equation*}
These integers bound both the terminal times and total sizes in~$\cD_q$. All jobs in~$\cD_q$ also have size at least~$2$. The initial job has size~$2$. Copying stretches sizes by factors $s_i\ge2$, and every heavy job has size $V\ge2H\ge2$. After flooding, all sizes lie in $[1/(2M),H_N]$, so $P(\hat I)\le2MH_N$.

\emph{Number of jobs.} Every instance in the support of~$\cD_N$ consists of jobs of size at least~$2$ and has total size at most~$H_N$, so it has at most $H_N/2$ jobs. Since every weight is at most~$1$, its total weight is at most~$H_N$. The additive term in~\cref{lem:flooding} is therefore at most $2H_N^2$.

Choose $L=4H_N^2+2$, a multiple of $1/(2M)$ because~$H_N$ and~$M$ are integers. Since~$\cD_N$ is $(2\rho,M)$-hard, the lemma gives $\E[F_A(\hat I)]-\rho\,\E[\OPT(\hat I)]\ge \rho(L/2-2H_N^2)=\rho\ge1$, as required. The stream adds $2M(4H_N^2+2)$ jobs. As $M\ge4$ and $H_N\ge2$, we obtain $|\hat I|\le H_N/2+2M(4H_N^2+2)\le(2MH_N)^2$.

\emph{Growth in~$\rho$.} We have $M\le5\rho$ and $k\le11\rho$. The explicit parameters of the iteration give
\begin{equation*}
  \log m=2k+2\log(2(k+1)M)=O(\rho),\qquad
  N=(M-2)2^k=2^{O(\rho)}\,.
\end{equation*}
Consequently,
\begin{equation*}
  \log W(\hat I)\le N\log(2m)=2^{O(\rho)},\qquad
  \log\log H_N=N\log(6km)+O(1)=2^{O(\rho)}\,.
\end{equation*}
Together with $P(\hat I)\le2MH_N$ and $|\hat I|\le(2MH_N)^2$, these estimates give the claimed bounds with an absolute constant~$C$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:quant}]
Let $W,P,n\ge16$, and let~$C$ be the constant of~\cref{thm:quant-refined}. Fix a randomized online algorithm~$\cA$ and set $\rho=\min\{\log\log W,\log\log\log P,\log\log\log n\}/C$. This is positive because $W,P,n\ge16$. If $\rho<1$, there is nothing to show, since no algorithm has competitive ratio less than~$1$.

Otherwise, let $\Gamma\ge0$. By~\cref{thm:quant-refined} with $\gamma=\Gamma+1$ and~\cref{lem:yao}, there is an instance~$\hat I$ with $W(\hat I)\le2^{2^{C\rho}}\le W$, $P(\hat I)\le2^{2^{2^{C\rho}}}\le P$, and $|\hat I|\le2^{2^{2^{C\rho}}}\le n$ such that $\E_{A\sim\cA}[F_A(\hat I)]\ge \rho\cdot\OPT(\hat I)+\Gamma+1$. Hence~$\cA$ is not $\rho$-competitive on this class of instances, proving the claimed lower bound.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A high-probability lower bound}\label{sec:probability}

The final averaging step also controls the probability of poor performance. Its capped online backlog has a small expected deficit, while its offline backlog is bounded by an average of independent bounded random variables.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:probability}
There is an absolute constant $C\ge1$ such that, for every $\rho\ge1$, $\gamma\ge0$, and randomized online algorithm~$\cA$, there is an instance~$I$ with
\begin{equation*}
  W(I)\le2^{2^{C\rho}},\qquad
  P(I)\le2^{2^{2^{C\rho}}},\qquad
  |I|\le2^{2^{2^{C\rho}}}
\end{equation*}
and
\begin{equation*}
  \Pr_{A\sim\cA}\bigl[F_A(I)\ge \rho\,\OPT(I)+\gamma\bigr]\ge1-2^{-\rho}\,.
\end{equation*}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Use the construction of~\cref{sec:iteration} with parameter~$2\rho$ in place of~$\rho$. Thus $M=\lceil8\rho\rceil$, $k=2M+1$, $\eta=2^{-k}$, and $m=(2(k+1)M/\eta)^2$. The last amplified distribution has gap at least $c_N=M/2$ and cap $(k+1)M$. By~\cref{lem:averaging}, the final terminal distribution~$\cD_N$ therefore satisfies
\begin{equation*}
  \E[\varphi_A^{M/2}(I,T)]\ge M/2-\eta/2
\end{equation*}
for every deterministic online algorithm~$A$. Since $M/2\ge4\rho$ and $0\le\varphi_A^{M/2}\le M/2$, Markov's inequality yields
\begin{equation*}
  \Pr\bigl[\varphi_A^M(I,T)<3\rho\bigr]
  \le\Pr\bigl[M/2-\varphi_A^{M/2}(I,T)>M/2-3\rho\bigr]
  \le\frac{\eta}{2\rho}\,.
\end{equation*}

For the offline bound, let $B_1,\dots,B_m$ be the values of~$U^*$ on the independent amplified samples entering the last averaging step. By~\cref{lem:amplification}, $0\le B_i\le2M$ and $\E[B_i]\le1$, so $\operatorname{Var}(B_i)\le\E[B_i^2]\le2M$. The offline schedule of~\cref{lem:copies}\ref{it:offline}, with weights divided by~$m$, gives $U^*(I,T)\le m^{-1}\sum_iB_i$. Independence and Chebyshev's inequality therefore imply
\begin{equation*}
  \Pr\bigl[U^*(I,T)>2\bigr]\le\frac{2M}{m}\,.
\end{equation*}
Consequently, for every deterministic~$A$, with probability at least $1-\eta/(2\rho)-2M/m\ge1-\eta$, we have
\begin{equation*}
  \varphi_A^M(I,T)\ge3\rho
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  U^*(I,T)\le2\,.
\end{equation*}
Here $2M/m=\eta^2/(2(k+1)^2M)\le\eta/2$, and $\eta=2^{-k}\le2^{-\rho}$.

Flood~$\cD_N$ with the length $L=4H_N^2+2$ used in the proof of~\cref{thm:quant-refined}. On the event above, the pointwise bounds of~\cref{lem:flooding} give
\begin{equation*}
  F_A(\hat I)-\rho\,\OPT(\hat I)
  \ge3\rho L-\rho\bigl(\tfrac52L+2H_N^2\bigr)
  =\rho(L/2-2H_N^2)=\rho\ge1\,.
\end{equation*}
Thus the resulting finite distribution~$\hat\cD$ satisfies $\Pr_{\hat I\sim\hat\cD}[F_A(\hat I)\ge \rho\,\OPT(\hat I)+1]\ge1-2^{-\rho}$ for every deterministic~$A$. The parameter bounds follow from~\cref{thm:quant-refined}, with an absolute constant enlarged to account for the use of~$2\rho$. Scaling all weights as in its proof replaces the additive term~$1$ by any~$\gamma\ge0$, preserving the parameter bounds and the probability guarantee for every deterministic algorithm.

Finally, average the indicators of these cost events over $A\sim\cA$. Since the instance distribution has finite support,
\begin{equation*}
  \E_{\hat I\sim\hat\cD}
  \left[\Pr_{A\sim\cA}\bigl[F_A(\hat I)\ge \rho\,\OPT(\hat I)+\gamma\bigr]\right]
  \ge1-2^{-\rho}\,.
\end{equation*}
For some instance in the support, the cost event therefore has probability at least $1-2^{-\rho}$.
\end{proof}

\section{Concluding remarks}\label{sec:conclusion}

We have shown that randomization does not allow a constant competitive ratio for online weighted flow time on a single machine. Our lower bound of $\Omega(\min\{\log\log W,\log\log\log P\})$ is exponentially weaker than the deterministic lower bound of Bansal and Chan~\cite{BansalC09}. The best known upper bound is $O(\log\min\{P,D,W\})$, achieved by a deterministic algorithm~\cite{AzarT18}. The weak dependence on~$W$ and~$P$ stems from the amplification step. Each round retains an additive increase of $\eta/2$, where $\eta=2^{-(2M+1)}$ is exponentially small in the cap~$M$. Our construction therefore uses $2^{\Theta(\rho)}$ rounds to reach the gap~$2\rho$. An amplification step whose improvement~$\eta$ is only polynomially small in the cap would lead to lower bounds that are polynomial in $\log W$ and $\log\log P$. More generally, it remains open whether randomized algorithms can beat the deterministic lower bound, for example by achieving a competitive ratio of $O(\log\log W)$.

In terms of the number~$n$ of jobs, our lower bound $\Omega(\log\log\log n)$ is weaker still, because flooding dominates the number of jobs. Each round multiplies the number of jobs by at most $m(k+1)$, so the instances in the support of~$\cD_N$ have at most $(m(k+1))^N=2^{2^{O(\rho)}}$ jobs. The stream must be long enough to make the cost before the terminal time negligible, and it consists of more than~$P$ jobs. A conversion of gaps at the terminal time into gaps in flow time that adds only polynomially many jobs would therefore give $\Omega(\log\log n)$. Whether a single machine admits an $O(\log n)$-competitive algorithm remains open. On $m\ge2$ machines, the lower bound of Chekuri, Khanna, and Zhu~\cite{ChekuriKZ01} rules out a polylogarithmic dependence on~$n$.
